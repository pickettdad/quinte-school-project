## 1. Ontario definitions of the relevant exceptionalities and how students are counted

### Takeaway
Ontario's five statutory categories (behavioural, communicational, intellectual, physical, multiple) use functional, education-oriented definitions rather than IQ cut-offs; the "Developmental Disability" and "Mild Intellectual Disability" definitions are distinguished mainly by whether the student can profit from a regular class with considerable modification (MID) versus needing a program designed for slow intellectual development (DD). Provincial counts come from the October OnSIS submission and show ~9,080 DD, ~10,005 MID, ~35,335 Autism and ~15,050 Multiple identified students in 2024-25, plus ~190,715 "non-identified" (IEP-only) students; the public dataset does not state a "counted once under primary exceptionality" rule, which should be confirmed.

### Cited Findings
- **[VERIFIED]** The Ministry's current definitions appear in *Special Education in Ontario, Kindergarten to Grade 12: Policy and Resource Guide* (2017), web edition published 31 Aug 2022, updated 19 Mar 2026; the page carries a notice that the 2017 document "may be outdated" and is under review. The Education Act identifies five categories: behavioural, communicational, intellectual, physical and multiple. — [Ontario.ca, Categories of exceptionalities](https://www.ontario.ca/document/special-education-ontario-policy-and-resource-guide-kindergarten-grade-12/categories)
- **[VERIFIED]** *Intellectual – Developmental Disability*: "a severe learning disorder characterized by: an inability to profit from a special education program for students with mild intellectual disabilities because of slow intellectual development; an ability to profit from a special education program that is designed to accommodate slow intellectual development; a limited potential for academic learning, independent social adjustment, and economic self-support." — [same source](https://www.ontario.ca/document/special-education-ontario-policy-and-resource-guide-kindergarten-grade-12/categories)
- **[VERIFIED]** *Intellectual – Mild Intellectual Disability*: "a learning disorder characterized by: an ability to profit educationally within a regular class with the aid of considerable curriculum modification and support services; an inability to profit educationally within a regular class because of slow intellectual development; a potential for academic learning, independent social adjustment, and economic self-support." — [same source](https://www.ontario.ca/document/special-education-ontario-policy-and-resource-guide-kindergarten-grade-12/categories)
- **[VERIFIED]** *Communication – Autism*: "a severe learning disorder that is characterized by: disturbances in rate of educational development; ability to relate to the environment; mobility; perception, speech, and language; lack of the representational symbolic behaviour that precedes language." — [same source](https://www.ontario.ca/document/special-education-ontario-policy-and-resource-guide-kindergarten-grade-12/categories)
- **[VERIFIED]** *Communication – Learning Disability* (revised definition per PPM 8, 2014): a neurodevelopmental disorder affecting ability to learn "in students who have assessed intellectual abilities that are at least in the average range," explicitly "not the result of ... intellectual disabilities." This is the key exclusion: a student with an intellectual disability cannot be identified LD. — [same source](https://www.ontario.ca/document/special-education-ontario-policy-and-resource-guide-kindergarten-grade-12/categories)
- **[VERIFIED]** *Multiple*: "a combination of learning or other disorders, impairments, or physical disabilities that is of such a nature as to require, for educational achievement, the services of one or more teachers holding qualifications in special education and the provision of support services appropriate for such disorders, impairments, or disabilities." The Ministry page gives no separate identification procedure for Multiple. — [same source](https://www.ontario.ca/document/special-education-ontario-policy-and-resource-guide-kindergarten-grade-12/categories)
- **[VERIFIED]** Ministry principle: "The determining factor for the provision of special education programs or services is not any specific diagnosed or undiagnosed medical condition, but rather the need of the individual student based on an individual assessment of strengths and needs"; a student "may be identified as exceptional within one or more of the categories." — [same source](https://www.ontario.ca/document/special-education-ontario-policy-and-resource-guide-kindergarten-grade-12/categories)
- **[VERIFIED]** Policy/Program Requirements 2024, s.3.4: "students with special education needs" covers every student receiving special education programs/services whether or not IPRC-identified; "Access to special education programs and/or services is not limited to students identified by an IPRC." Identification/placement process is in O. Reg. 181/98. — [Ontario Schools K-12 PPR 2024, ch. 3](https://www.ontario.ca/document/ontario-schools-kindergarten-grade-12-policy-and-program-requirements/meeting-needs)
- **[VERIFIED]** Board-level operational criteria vary and are not in the Ministry definitions. Example: OCDSB Developmental Disability Program criteria require "Diagnosis of an Intellectual Disability-Moderate to Severe (i.e., at or below the 0.1% level) incorporating assessments of both cognitive and adaptive functioning"; provisional diagnoses acceptable especially under age 10. — [OCDSB DDP criteria sheet](https://www.ocdsb.ca/download/485485)
- **[VERIFIED]** Trillium Lakelands DSB: DD identification requires a formal assessment "under the supervision of a medical doctor, a psychologist or psychological associate" reviewed by board psych staff; disability must be present before 18, permanent, affect learning. — [TLDSB SEAC Developmental](https://www.tldsb.ca/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/SEAC-Developmental.pdf)
- **[VERIFIED]** Kawartha Pine Ridge DSB (neighbouring board) reproduces Education Act definitions verbatim and states that the "identification and placement which best facilitates the student's successful learning is determined by the Committee." No numeric thresholds published. — [KPR Standard 10](https://kprschools.ca/media/w52dpshs/standard-10-categories-and-definitions-of-exceptionalities.pdf)
- **[VERIFIED]** Provincial special education enrolment by exceptionality, 2024-25 (OnSIS October count, public/Catholic boards only; counts <10 suppressed, totals rounded to nearest 5): Developmental Disability 4,040 elementary / 5,040 secondary / 9,080 total; Mild Intellectual Disability 3,935 / 6,070 / 10,005; Autism 24,535 / 10,800 / 35,335; Multiple 7,450 / 7,600 / 15,050; Learning Disability 19,595 / 30,560 / 50,160; Non-identified 114,555 / 76,160 / 190,715; provincial total 204,155 / 158,510 / 362,670. — [Ontario Data Catalogue, 2024-25 file](https://data.ontario.ca/dataset/b498b7ab-c6d0-4791-b8bd-c25c58e5bdbf/resource/d644aa6c-371d-48ba-aaed-e6c560abfc72/download/special_education_enrolment_by_exceptionality_2024-2025_en.txt); dataset description at [data.ontario.ca](https://data2.ontario.ca/dataset/special-education-enrolment-by-exceptionality)
- **[VERIFIED]** The dataset excludes private schools, school authorities, provincial/demonstration schools and ECPP facilities; "non-identified" = receiving special education programs/services without an IPRC identification; "boards may differ in how they identify and place students." The dataset page does **not** state that each student is counted once under a primary exceptionality. — [open.canada.ca mirror](https://open.canada.ca/data/en/dataset/b498b7ab-c6d0-4791-b8bd-c25c58e5bdbf)
- **[VERIFIED]** IPRC placement options and sequence: the IPRC may choose "a regular class with indirect support," "with resource assistance," "with withdrawal assistance," "a special education class with partial integration," or "a special education class full time"; under O. Reg. 181/98 s.17(1) it "must consider placement in a regular class with appropriate special education services" first and choose it if "satisfied that placement in a regular class would meet the student's needs and is consistent with parental preferences"; a special-class decision must include written "reasons for placing the student in a special education class" (cl. 18(2)(c)); review "at least once in each school year." — [Ontario.ca, Identification and placement](https://www.ontario.ca/document/special-education-ontario-policy-and-resource-guide-kindergarten-grade-12/identification)
- **[VERIFIED]** O. Reg. 298 s.31 special education class maxima (as summarized in the 2017 Guide): DD 10; MID 12 (primary) / 16 (junior-intermediate); autism/aphasia 6; multiple exceptionalities with no dominant exceptionality 6; mixed exceptionalities 16; emotional/severe LD 8; blind/Deaf/speech-language 10; hard of hearing/low vision/physical 12. — [Ontario.ca, Summary of requirements](https://www.ontario.ca/document/special-education-ontario-policy-and-resource-guide-kindergarten-grade-12/summary); the s.31 maxima were confirmed as still in force in a 2025 arbitration note (autism 6, DD 10, "in place for over 40 years"). — [Hicks Morley, 15 Sep 2025](https://www.hicksmorley.com/2025/09/15/school-boards-take-note-arbitrator-finds-class-size-maxima-still-apply-to-mainstream-classes-with-special-needs-students-expecting-to-receive-less-than-full-credit/)
- **[VERIFIED]** Education Act s.33(1): a person is qualified to be a resident pupil "until the last school day in June in the year in which the person attains the age of 21 years." — [Avon Maitland DSB AP 302 quoting the Act](https://www.amdsb.ca/apps/pages/ap302)

### Inferences
- **[PROPOSAL]** For a private school, the Ministry categories matter chiefly as a shared vocabulary with referring families, boards and the DSO; they are not an admission standard. Because the DD/MID line is functional (can the student profit in a regular class with considerable modification?), the school's suitability framework (section 9) should be built on learning/communication/social/support needs, consistent with the Ministry's own "need, not diagnosis" principle.
- **[ESTIMATE]** DD + MID identified students ≈ 19,085 province-wide (≈5.3% of all students with special education needs; ≈0.9% of Ontario's roughly 2 million publicly funded students). Applying that share to HPEDSB's enrolment (not retrieved here; → feasibility demand stream) would give an order-of-magnitude local population; add a share of "Multiple" and autism students whose profile overlaps.
- **[ESTIMATE]** Secondary DD counts exceed elementary (5,040 vs 4,040) even though secondary enrolment is smaller, consistent with later formal identification and students remaining to age 21. Secondary MID (6,070) is also higher than elementary (3,935). This suggests the demand pool is weighted toward ages 12–21.

### Gaps
- Board-level counts for HPEDSB and Algonquin & Lakeshore Catholic DSB are not in the provincial file (it is a provincial summary); board-level data are in board Special Education Plans, which were not retrievable here. **[CONFIRM]**
- Whether OnSIS counts a student once under a single (primary) exceptionality: not stated on the dataset page; the Ministry says a student may be identified in "one or more" categories, and "Multiple" exists as its own category, which suggests one category per IPRC statement, but this needs confirmation from OnSIS reporting instructions. **[CONFIRM]**
- Current e-Laws text of O. Reg. 181/98 and O. Reg. 298 could not be fetched (JavaScript-gated); figures above rely on Ministry summaries and a 2025 legal note.

## 2. Research evidence on instruction for students with intellectual disability, and on social outcomes in specialized vs inclusive settings

### Takeaway
There is consistent (mostly single-case and quasi-experimental, plus one large longitudinal RCT-style trial) evidence that explicit, systematic, small-group instruction with high dosage over years produces measurable literacy and numeracy gains for students with mild-to-moderate ID; the evidence on *where* that instruction happens (inclusive vs separate) is weak, non-causal, and mixed, with the best recent syntheses finding small, non-significant effects favouring inclusion for academics and psychosocial adjustment, while individual studies show inclusive settings yield more friends and better language/literacy, and special settings yield higher self-reported happiness, more interpersonal relationships for older teens and less loneliness in some samples but more in others.

### Cited Findings — instruction
- **[VERIFIED]** Allor, Mathes, Roberts et al. (2014, *Exceptional Children*): 4-year longitudinal study, IES-funded ($3M), 141 children (76 intervention, 65 business-as-usual), IQ 40–80, most entering ~age 7, diagnoses incl. Down syndrome, autism, Williams syndrome. Intervention: "Early Interventions in Reading," 40–50 minutes daily in groups of 1–4 per teacher, delivered by 6 certified special-education teachers and 4 part-time certified general-education teachers. Students with mild/moderate ID could read independently at a first-grade level, some higher; intervention group significantly outperformed comparison on several reading measures; IQ did not reliably predict achievement. — [News-Medical summary of SMU study, 22 May 2014](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20140522/Four-year-educational-study-offers-hope-for-children-with-intellectual-disability.aspx)
- **[VERIFIED]** Alnahdi (2015) systematic review, 16 studies: both comprehensive approaches (phonics + direct instruction) and single-skill approaches (sight words) were "effective in teaching reading" to students with ID; students "typically need a lot of time and intensive practice." No quality appraisal reported. — [ASHA Evidence Maps summary](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/1ab638ff-61c8-4475-b834-a65e28eec7a4)
- **[VERIFIED]** Hill (2016) systematic review, 11 studies (2001–2013), students aged 6–21 with ID: phonics-based interventions effective; "systematic and explicit instruction was common to the effective interventions." Effect sizes/dosage not reported in abstract. — [ASHA Evidence Maps summary](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/d30d82f0-8436-4eb8-aca4-292df3e11354)
- **[VERIFIED]** Spooner, Knight, Browder & Smith (2011, *Remedial and Special Education*): review of 2003–2010 literature, 18 single-case experiments meeting Horner et al. quality indicators. Evidence-based practices for teaching academics to students with severe developmental disabilities: task-analytic instruction with systematic prompting and feedback (7 studies, 29 participants); time delay for discrete responses (13 studies); 61.1% of studies in special education classrooms, 38.9% in general education, 33.3% community settings. — [PDF](https://intelligentlives.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/Evidence-Based-Practices-for-Teaching-Academic-Skills-to-Students-with-Severe-Developmental-Disabilities.pdf)
- **[VERIFIED]** Hudson, Rivera & Grady (2018) update of Browder et al. (2008) mathematics meta-analysis: adds 29 studies; five evidence-based practices for students with significant cognitive disabilities: systematic instruction, in-vivo instruction, system of least prompts, constant time delay, task-analytic instruction. — [TEA research summary](https://spedsupport.tea.texas.gov/resource-library/complex-access-needs-research-summaries/mathematics)
- **[VERIFIED]** Education Endowment Foundation, *Special Educational Needs in Mainstream Schools* guidance report (first published 20 Mar 2020): five recommendations incl. "Complement high quality teaching with carefully selected small-group and one-to-one interventions" and "Work effectively with teaching assistants." Five strategies with "relatively strong evidence": flexible grouping, cognitive/metacognitive strategies, explicit instruction, technology, scaffolding. On TAs: they "should supplement the classroom teacher, not replace them," should not be "an informal teaching resource for low-attaining pupils," can deliver structured small-group/1:1 interventions with training and evidence-based materials; poor deployment "can harm pupils' learning and wellbeing." Explicit instruction = demonstration → guided practice → independent practice, small steps, examples/non-examples, anticipating misconceptions. — [EEF guidance report PDF](https://dera.ioe.ac.uk/37846/1/EEF_Special_Educational_Needs_in_Mainstream_Schools_Guidance_Report.pdf); [EEF page](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports/send)
- **[VERIFIED]** EEF Teaching & Learning Toolkit, peer tutoring: +6 months average progress; evidence rated high (145 studies); very low cost; lower-attaining pupils benefit more; "some evidence suggests pupils with special educational needs make some of the biggest gains"; cross-age tutoring works best with an age gap under three years and when tutors/tutees are not matched by attainment; most effective for review/consolidation, 4–5 sessions/week for up to 10 weeks; training for staff and tutors "essential." — [EEF Toolkit, Peer tutoring](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/peer-tutoring)

### Cited Findings — inclusive vs specialized placement
- **[VERIFIED]** Dalgaard, Bondebjerg, Viinholt & Filges, Campbell Systematic Review (Dec 2022, Vol 18 No 4): 94 non-randomised studies from 19 countries met criteria; 79 excluded for critical risk of bias; 15 used in meta-analysis. SMD (inclusion vs segregated): psychosocial adjustment 0.20 (95% CI −0.01 to 0.42); language/literacy 0.04 (−0.27 to 0.35); math 0.05 (−0.16 to 0.26) — none significant; conclusion that it is "very unlikely that inclusion in general increases or decreases learning and psychosocial adjustment"; quality of evidence low, 14/15 studies serious risk of bias; no separate ID results. — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/b90de3a9e36f4ce38d609eedb0dc0275)
- **[VERIFIED]** Dell'Anna, Pellegrini, Ianes & Vivanet (2020, *Int J Disability, Development and Education*): systematic review of 18 studies on moderate/severe/complex disabilities; only 7 compared inclusive and special settings; one quasi-experiment. Findings: three quantitative studies found better literacy in inclusive settings; Dessemontet et al. (2012) no difference in math; Matzen et al. (2010) instructional time 85–96% inclusive vs 48–56% self-contained, downtime 0.3–4% vs 18–27%; Olsson et al. (2018) 47% of students in inclusive schools had friends vs 31% in special schools, but happiness higher in special settings (62% vs 33%) and discussion participation higher in special schools (31% vs 23%); Hardiman et al. (2009) advantage for inclusion in personal/social responsibility, no difference in social adjustment, more teacher-rated hyperactivity in inclusive schools; Matzen found self-stimulation and self-injury more in self-contained settings. Authors: evidence "only moderately in favour of inclusion for learning outcomes," social participation "less reassuring, showing partial marginalisation and isolation," evidence base "inadequate." — [PDF](https://iris.unica.it/bitstream/11584/301821/1/Learning%20Social%20and%20Psychological%20Outcomes%20of%20Students%20with%20Moderate%20Severe%20and%20Complex%20Disabilities%20in%20Inclusive%20Education%20A%20Systematic%20Review.pdf)
- **[VERIFIED]** Dessemontet, Bless & Morin (2012, *J Intellectual Disability Research*): 68 children with ID (34 fully included with support, 34 in special schools), two school years: included children made "slightly more progress in literacy," no difference in mathematics or adaptive behaviour; small effect; quasi-experimental. — [summary](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/school-classroom/effects-of-inclusion-on-the-academic-achievement-and-adaptive-behaviou/)
- **[VERIFIED]** Oh-Young & Filler (2015, *Research in Developmental Disabilities* 47:80–92): meta-analysis of 24 studies (1980–2013) found significant differences (p<0.0001) favouring more integrated placements on academic and social measures; disability breakdown and effect sizes not in abstract. — [UNLV repository](https://oasis.library.unlv.edu/ecs_fac_articles/8)
- **[VERIFIED]** Buckley, Bird, Sacks & Archer (2006, *Down Syndrome Research and Practice* 9(3):54–67): Hampshire UK teens with Down syndrome, 18 mainstream vs 23 special school (after excluding the 5 least able special-school students); parent-report survey, placement by residence not randomised. Mainstream ≈2y6m ahead in expressive language, ≈3y4m in written language; intelligibility to strangers 78% vs 56%; no difference in Vineland socialisation or daily living; special-school teens scored higher on Interpersonal Relationships (more likely to have a boyfriend/girlfriend, special friend, club membership); significant behaviour difficulties 12% mainstream vs 32% special. Authors caution small samples, age differences, selection effects and an unusually well-supported inclusion region. — [DSE library](https://www.down-syndrome.org/en-us/library/research-practice/09/3/comparison-mainstream-special-education-teenagers-down-syndrome-implications-parents-teachers)
- **[VERIFIED]** Heiman (2000, *J Intellectual & Developmental Disability* 25(1):1–12): 121 adolescents with mild ID in special schools, 189 in self-contained classes in mainstream schools, 265 without disability: special-school students "tended to have fewer friends," "responded more passively, and felt lonelier than students in the other groups," and most met friends only at school. — [Open University of Israel record](https://cris.openu.ac.il/en/publications/friendship-quality-among-children-in-three-educational-settings/)
- **[VERIFIED]** Schoop-Kasteler & Müller (2020, *J Research in Special Educational Needs* 20(2):130–145): systematic review of 36 studies; students with ID in special needs classrooms "develop and maintain differentiated peer relationships," varying with cognitive skills and classroom type; far less research exists on special-classroom peer relations than on inclusive classrooms. — [Fribourg repository](https://folia.unifr.ch/unifr/documents/308505)
- **[VERIFIED]** Fuchs, Gilmour & Wanzek (2025, *J Learning Disabilities*, online Feb 2025): review of 50+ years of placement research concludes evidence is "extremely weak" and "fundamentally flawed" by selection bias (students in separate settings have more severe disabilities and lower prior achievement); the 1991 NLTS inclusion advantage disappeared after adjusting for prior achievement; recommends focusing on the intervention the student needs, which for many may require intensive specialized instruction in a separate setting, while mild-needs students should stay in general classrooms. Critics (Newman, SRI) note omission of recent well-designed studies showing post-school benefits of inclusion; others note studies of students with the most severe disabilities favoured inclusion. Fuchs did not study social/psychological outcomes. — [Hechinger Report, 2025](https://hechingerreport.org/proof-points-special-education-inclusion-research-flawed/)
- **[VERIFIED]** Gilmour (2018, *Education Next* 18(4)): "only weak evidence that inclusion confers benefits on SWDs"; Hanushek et al. Texas study with better selection controls found SWDs performed neither better nor worse in regular vs special settings; a Fuchs fractions study found the math gap about twice as large in regular classrooms as in intensive special-education instruction; peer-effect studies are correlational and concentrated on EBD, not ID. — [Education Next](https://www.educationnext.org/has-inclusion-gone-too-far-weighing-effects-students-with-disabilities-peers-teachers/)
- **[VERIFIED]** Canadian policy context: Supreme Court of Canada, *Eaton v. Brant County Board of Education* [1997] 1 SCR 241, held that integration "can be either a benefit or a burden" depending on whether the student can benefit, that a presumption in favour of integration would disadvantage students who need special education to achieve equality, and that placing Emily Eaton in a special class against parental wishes did not violate Charter equality rights; the placement test is the child's best interests. — [OHRC consultation paper](https://www.ohrc.on.ca/en/consultation-paper-education-and-disability-human-rights-issues-ontarios-education-system/iii-human)
- **[VERIFIED]** OHRC 2018 policy (per People for Education, 4 Sep 2018): place students in a regular classroom first; where not possible, include them with non-disabled peers where possible; end the practice of asking parents to keep students home for part of the day. — [People for Education](https://peopleforeducation.ca/?p=7480)
- **[VERIFIED]** Inclusion Canada's vision is that people with ID are "fully included with their peers in regular education"; lists "segregated classrooms" among discriminatory practices; claims "60% of children and youth with an intellectual disability are denied inclusive, quality education in Canada" (no source/year given). — [Inclusion Canada](https://inclusioncanada.ca/campaign/inclusive-education/)
- **[VERIFIED]** Canadian Down Syndrome Society position (revised June 2020): all students have a right to be included in "age-appropriate, regular classes" at their neighbourhood schools, while CDSS "respects the right of families to make personal decisions about" their child's education. — [CDSS position statement PDF](https://Www.Cdss.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/Inclusive-Education-Position-Statement.pdf)
- **[VERIFIED]** People for Education 2018: 17% of elementary and 27% of secondary students per school receive special education support; 58% of elementary and 48% of secondary principals had recommended a student with special needs not attend for the full day (73% citing safety); average supported-students-to-EA ratio 19:1 elementary, 61:1 secondary; 93% of elementary schools have assessment waitlists; only 72% of rural elementary schools have a full-time special education teacher vs 92% urban. — [P4E 2018 special education report](https://peopleforeducation.ca/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/Special-education.pdf)

### Inferences
- **[PROPOSAL]** The evidence most robustly supports *what* the school does (explicit, systematic, high-dosage small-group literacy and numeracy over multiple years, delivered by trained teachers with EAs used in structured roles, plus task-analysed functional skills taught in vivo) rather than *where* it does it. The feasibility report should frame the school's case on instructional intensity and continuity, not on an assertion that congregated settings produce better outcomes per se, because the best syntheses do not support that claim (nor its opposite).
- **[PROPOSAL]** The social-outcome literature points to specific risks a congregated school must design against: smaller friendship pools (Heiman; Olsson), reduced instructional time and more downtime in self-contained classes (Matzen), and loss of exposure to typical peer language models (Buckley). Mitigations with some evidence: structured peer tutoring with a <3-year age gap, shared activities with community/typical-peer groups, maximizing scheduled instructional minutes, and explicit social-skills teaching. Conversely, the literature suggests advantages a well-run specialized setting can claim: higher self-reported happiness (Olsson), more interpersonal relationships for older teens (Buckley), and more instruction matched to level (Fuchs).
- **[ESTIMATE]** Allor's design (40–50 min daily reading in groups of 1–4 over 4 years) is a usable benchmark for the proposed school's literacy block: for an opening cohort of 12, that implies 3–4 concurrent small groups, i.e., at least 3 instructional adults during the literacy block.

### Gaps
- No Ontario- or Canada-specific outcome study comparing congregated DD/MID classes with inclusive placement was found; Canadian evidence is positional (OHRC, Inclusion Canada, CDSS) rather than empirical.
- Effect sizes for the Alnahdi, Hill and Oh-Young reviews were not accessible (ASHA pages returned errors; abstracts only).
- No study was found on outcomes of *small private* specialized schools (as opposed to public special schools or self-contained classes); all comparative evidence is from public systems.

## 3. How specialized schools group students and handle elementary/secondary separation, teen programming and cross-age roles

### Takeaway
Specialized schools consistently group by learning profile ("pathways" or "levels") rather than strictly by grade, while keeping age bands for social appropriateness; several keep a distinct post-16/secondary department with work-oriented programming. No source explicitly addresses preventing "older students as caregivers," but peer-tutoring evidence (small age gap, structured tasks, trained tutors, reciprocal benefit) and Ontario board practice (separate elementary and secondary programs, Certificate of Accomplishment track with work experience from ~16) give a defensible design basis.

### Cited Findings
- **[VERIFIED]** Ash Lea School (England, SLD): four pathways (pre-formal, informal, semi-formal, formal); "The pathways are not defined by age, but by learning needs and attainment"; pupils can move between pathways; post-16 programme focuses on core skills, "life after Ash Lea," and personal/social enrichment. — [Ash Lea curriculum pathways](https://www.ashlea.notts.sch.uk/curriculum-pathways/)
- **[VERIFIED]** Lisburne School (Stockport, primary special): pupils enter in EYFS then follow formal (subject-specific, 17 classes) or pre-formal (Engagement Model, 4 classes) routes; placement "based on several factors, including the child's level of need, attainment, and engagement"; pathways not fixed. — [Lisburne School](https://www.lisburneschool.stockport.sch.uk/page/our-learning-pathways/121498)
- **[VERIFIED]** Hazel Court School (Eastbourne, ages 11–19, 123 on roll incl. 53 in sixth form, Ofsted Outstanding June 2024): pupils placed on one of three curriculum routes; Year 10 pupils who are ready access a "bridging curriculum" to functional skills qualifications; all sixth-form pupils who attempted it achieved an accredited English qualification. — [Ofsted report](https://files.ofsted.gov.uk/v1/file/50252955)
- **[VERIFIED]** Bettridge School (Gloucestershire, SLD/PMLD, 134 places ages 2–19 incl. 24 post-16): "Pupils are placed in their chronological year group"; separate post-16 centre for SLD/PMLD and severe challenging behaviour often linked to autism. — [Commissioning brief 2017-18](https://www.gloucestershire.gov.uk/media/e1jbjh53/bettridge-school-commissioning-brief-2017-2018.pdf)
- **[VERIFIED]** Janus Academy (Calgary, autism, grades 1–12, 95 students, classes 8–14): "Students are grouped by compatible needs rather than strictly by grade," each on an Individual Program Plan, modified Alberta curriculum. (school-reported) — [OurKids profile](https://www.ourkids.net/school/janus-academy/1315)
- **[VERIFIED]** Summit School (Montreal, ages 4–21): elementary students "are grouped for academic subjects by level and move at their own pace"; CASP Jr & Sr covers ages 6–15; Modified Secondary Cycle 1 is fully individualized with no credits toward a leaving certificate; "Challenges" (social integration, post-secondary-age) and Work-Oriented Training Path (WOTP, 15+, ends with a MELS Pre-Work Training certificate); classes ~10–12 with minimum 2 staff. (school-reported) — [Summit programs](https://summit-school.com/programs/); [Summit admissions](https://summit-school.com/admissions/)

Intellectual – Developmental Disability," alternative non-credit courses, Certificate of Accomplishment) and a Community Integration Program (credits + Leveled Literacy Intervention + work experience, leading to an OSSC with 14 credits); Bayside SS has 2 LSP and 1 CIP class. — [HPEDSB LSP](https://www.hpedsb.on.ca/about_us/contact/schools/school_groups/bayside/life_skills_program__lsp_); [Bayside special education](https://bss.hpedsb.on.ca/about_bss/special_education)

- **[VERIFIED]** Peer tutoring design evidence (EEF): age gap under three years; tutors benefit through revisiting skills; structured tasks and questioning frames; training essential; best for consolidation; see section 2. — [EEF Toolkit](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/peer-tutoring)
- **[VERIFIED]** EEF SEND guidance: TAs and by extension any helper should "supplement ... not replace" the teacher and not be an informal resource for low attainers — a principle that argues against using older students as de facto support staff. — [EEF guidance](https://dera.ioe.ac.uk/37846/1/EEF_Special_Educational_Needs_in_Mainstream_Schools_Guidance_Report.pdf)

### Inferences
- **[PROPOSAL]** A workable grouping model for a 12–30-student school: two or three "learning pathway" groups for core literacy/numeracy (by current skill and communication level, re-assessed termly, with movement allowed), nested inside two age bands (roughly ages 6–13 "elementary/junior" and 14–21 "secondary/transition") used for homeroom, social curriculum, personal-care privacy, and community/work programming. This mirrors Ash Lea/Lisburne (pathway by need) and Bettridge/HPEDSB (chronological band and distinct secondary program).
- **[PROPOSAL]** Teen-specific programming should be distinct from the elementary day: a timetable with work-related learning, travel/independence training, personal finance, relationships/health, and community placements (Storefront, Halton CPP and Summit WOTP as templates), with the Certificate of Accomplishment or OSSC as the credential depending on pathway (see section 5).
- **[PROPOSAL]** Cross-age contact should be structured as *reciprocal, time-limited, trained peer tutoring or shared activities* (clubs, student market, garden) with a ≤3-year age gap where tutoring occurs, never as unpaid supervision or personal care. Written policy should state that students are never responsible for another student's safety, toileting, feeding, medication or behaviour, and that adult:student ratios are calculated without counting older students. (No source directly addresses this; this is a design inference from the EEF TA principles and peer-tutoring evidence.)

### Gaps
- No published policy or research specifically on preventing older students with ID from being cast as caregivers in special schools was found.
- Class-size and adult:pupil ratios in the English pathway schools were not stated in the pages retrieved (see section 8 for sector averages).

## 4. Practical/life-skills curriculum: kitchens, gardens, crafts, markets, community participation — concrete examples, supervision, food safety, voluntariness

### Takeaway
Student-run cafés, coffee carts, gardens and enterprise stalls are common in both Ontario board programs and UK special schools; they are typically run by a special-education teacher plus 1–2 EAs/job coaches, framed as "pre-co-op" or work-related learning, priced to cover supplies, and linked to IEP goals rather than accreditation. Ontario public health treats any ongoing on-site food preparation as a food premises under O. Reg. 493/17 (certified food handler on site, hand-wash sink, notification to the health unit), with low-risk pre-packaged programs exempt from some requirements; UK CLEAPSS gives the most concrete classroom-cooking supervision numbers (≤10 children using sharp tools, ≤3 heating food under very close supervision, extra adult recommended).

- **[VERIFIED]** Ontario food premises rules as applied to schools (York Region Public Health summary of O. Reg. 493/17): a school is a food premises when food is made available; an ongoing program involving preparation (e.g., "cutting cheese or cooking eggs") is a food service premises requiring at least one Certified Food Handler on site at all times, a designated hand-wash sink, commercial dishwashing, notification to the health unit before starting, and inspections (1–3/year by risk). Programs preparing only low-risk, ready-to-eat, pre-packaged foods are exempt from the dedicated hand-wash sink, commercial dishwashing and certified-handler requirements (though handwashing and sanitizing are still required). Student Nutrition Ontario's free Safe Food Handling course is accepted only for nutrition-program workers. — [York Region, Food Safety in Schools](https://www.york.ca/health/food-safety/food-safety-schools)
- **[VERIFIED]** Simcoe Muskoka health unit: all schools must comply with O. - **[VERIFIED]** CLEAPSS P183 "Cooking in a classroom" (Feb 2024, UK): up to 10 children may prepare food with sharp cutting equipment at one time; up to 3 may heat food, needing "very close supervision"; an extra briefed adult recommended; hotplate dishes under 10 minutes; avoid raw fish/meat; hair tied back, sleeves rolled, hands washed before and after; children should not taste during preparation; adults clean cutting equipment; allergy checks and ingredient letters home. - **[VERIFIED]** North Carolina DEH memo "Food Handling for Special Needs Students" (31 Oct 2005): best-practice (non-enforceable) recommendations that food generally be prepared in a permitted area; teachers wash hands before assisting; separate hand-wash sink for food handling vs diapering/toileting; family-supplied appliances labelled, sanitized and stored in the lunchroom. — [NC DHHS memo](https://ehs-prod.dph.ncdhhs.gov/hhccehb/cehu/schools/FoodHandlingSpecialNeedsStudents-111405.pdf)
- **[VERIFIED]** Project SEARCH staffing benchmark for work-based learning: cohort of ~10 students with one teacher and two skills trainers (≈1:3–4), three internship rotations over a 10-month year, embedded in a host business. — [Holland Bloorview policy brief, Feb 2025](https://hollandbloorview.ca/pdf/project-search-policy-brief)

### Inferences
- **[PROPOSAL]** Supervision benchmarks for the feasibility report: kitchen/cooking sessions at no more than 1 adult : 3 students when heat or knives are in use (CLEAPSS ≤3 heating per adult; Oakfield 1:1 "when necessary"); garden/craft sessions at ~1:4–5 with two adults minimum (Lakeside started 3 students : 2 TAs); café/market service at ~1:3–4 with one adult handling cash reconciliation and one on food hygiene (BCN Café used 2 teachers + 2 EAs for a small group).
- **[PROPOSAL]** Food-safety design: treat the teaching kitchen as a food premises from day one — notify Hastings Prince Edward Public Health before opening, have at least one Certified Food Handler on staff per session (the course is inexpensive and portable), install a dedicated hand-wash sink separate from any personal-care area, and keep student-made food for *internal* consumption or a low-risk (baked goods, packaged) market menu until the health unit confirms the premises classification. **[CONFIRM]** with Hastings Prince Edward Public Health (→ legal stream for regulatory detail).
- **[PROPOSAL]** Keeping participation educational and voluntary: tie every kitchen/garden/market task to an IEP alternative-expectation goal and record progress (Lakeside, Greenside RARPA model); price products only to cover supplies (BCN) or route surplus to a student-chosen purpose (Clara Hughes); rotate roles so no student is a fixed "worker"; include an opt-out/alternative activity and student choice of role each term; avoid any arrangement in which student labour subsidizes school operations, which would blur the line between education and unpaid work.
- **[ESTIMATE]** Evidence quality for these activities is descriptive (case studies, awards, parent reports), not controlled; the feasibility report should present them as well-established practice with face validity and IEP alignment, not as evidence-based interventions.

- No Ontario source specifies supervision ratios for cooking classes with students with ID; CLEAPSS (UK) is the only numeric guidance found.
- No source documented how any of the café/market programs handle allergen control, cash-handling policy, or liability insurance (→ legal/funding streams).

## 5. Secondary pathways in Ontario: OSSD, OSSC, Certificate of Accomplishment, alternative (K) programming, and implications of granting or not granting credits

### Takeaway
Ontario's three secondary credentials are the OSSD (30 credits; 17 compulsory + 13 optional for students entering Grade 9 in 2024-25 or later; literacy requirement; 40 community hours; two online credits; new financial-literacy requirement), the OSSC (14 credits: 7 compulsory + 7 optional, on request at 18), and the Certificate of Accomplishment (no credit threshold, for students leaving at 18 without an OSSD/OSSC, transcript attached, IEP may be included). Alternative expectations are by definition outside the Ontario Curriculum, carry K-codes and "are not eligible for course credit"; modified-expectation credit eligibility is a principal's decision. Most Ontario DD programs are non-credit and lead to the Certificate of Accomplishment; MID programs often blend credits toward an OSSC. Adult eligibility (DSO, Passport, ODSP) does not depend on credentials; CICE college programs require OSSD/OSSC *or* age 19+, so a non-credit pathway does not close that door.

### Cited Findings
- **[VERIFIED]** OSSD (PPR 2024 s.6.1): 30 credits — 18 compulsory/12 optional for students entering Grade 9 in Sept 2023 or earlier; 17 compulsory/13 optional for 2024-25 entrants onward; at least two online credits; literacy requirement via OSSLT, OSSLC or adjudication (s.6.1.3); Financial Literacy Graduation Requirement (70% in Grade 10 Career Studies component) for 2025-26 Grade 9 entrants (s.6.1.4); 40 hours community involvement (s.6.1.6, PPM 124). Document published 23 Aug 2024, updated 14 Jan 2026; replaces 2016 edition. — [PPR 2024 ch. 6](https://www.ontario.ca/document/ontario-schools-kindergarten-grade-12-policy-and-program-requirements/diploma-and-certificate-requirements-related-procedures); [PPR 2024 front matter](https://www.ontario.ca/document/ontario-schools-kindergarten-grade-12-policy-and-program-requirements-2024)
- **[VERIFIED]** OSSC (s.6.3): granted on request to students leaving school at 18 without an OSSD; minimum 14 credits — 7 compulsory (2 English, 1 math, 1 science, 1 Canadian history or geography, 1 health & PE, 1 arts/computer studies/technological education) and 7 optional; substitution rules in s.6.2 apply. — [PPR 2024 ch. 6](https://www.ontario.ca/document/ontario-schools-kindergarten-grade-12-policy-and-program-requirements/diploma-and-certificate-requirements-related-procedures)
- **[VERIFIED]** Certificate of Accomplishment (s.6.4): for students leaving at 18 without OSSD or OSSC; recognizes achievement "especially for students going directly to further training or employment"; must be accompanied by the Ontario Student Transcript; "For students with an Individual Education Plan, a copy of the plan may also be included"; students who return to complete further credit or non-credit courses (including modified/alternative special education courses) have the transcript updated but do not get a new certificate. — [same](https://www.ontario.ca/document/ontario-schools-kindergarten-grade-12-policy-and-program-requirements/diploma-and-certificate-requirements-related-procedures)
- **[VERIFIED]** Students with an IEP who are not working toward an OSSD may be exempted from the OSSLT/OSSLC with parental consent and principal approval (s.6.1.3). — [same](https://www.ontario.ca/document/ontario-schools-kindergarten-grade-12-policy-and-program-requirements/diploma-and-certificate-requirements-related-procedures)
- **[VERIFIED]** Accommodations vs modifications vs alternative expectations (s.3.4.1): accommodations leave grade expectations unchanged; modifications change number/complexity or use another grade's expectations, and at secondary "the principal decides whether achieving modified expectations makes a student eligible for course credit"; alternative expectations cover "knowledge and skills that are not represented in the Ontario Curriculum" and "are not eligible for course credit at the secondary level" (cross-ref s.7.4). — [PPR 2024 ch. 3](https://www.ontario.ca/document/ontario-schools-kindergarten-grade-12-policy-and-program-requirements/meeting-needs)
- **[VERIFIED]** K-codes (s.7.4): "Course codes for alternative (non-credit) courses begin with 'K'"; these are individualized, documented in the IEP, and usually prepare students for daily living, employment or community living. One credit = a course scheduled for a minimum of 110 hours with a final mark ≥50% (s.7.1). Locally developed course policy "also applies to inspected private schools" (s.7.3.1). — [PPR 2024 ch. 7](https://www.ontario.ca/document/ontario-schools-kindergarten-grade-12-policy-and-program-requirements/secondary-school-courses-and-related-procedures)
- **[VERIFIED]** IEP content requirements (s.3.4.2): strengths/needs/expectations; programs and services; "a statement of the methods by which the student's progress will be assessed"; a PPM 156 transition plan. — [PPR 2024 ch. 3](https://www.ontario.ca/document/ontario-schools-kindergarten-grade-12-policy-and-program-requirements/meeting-needs)
- **[VERIFIED]** Teacher certification (→ legal stream, educational implication only): Ontario private school "principals and teachers ... don't need to be certified by the Ontario College of Teachers"; only private high schools that pass a Ministry inspection may grant OSSD credits; elementary private schools are not inspected and need not follow the Ontario curriculum (page updated 2 Oct 2026). — [Ontario.ca Private schools](https://www.ontario.ca/page/private-schools-0); the 2013 Private Schools Policy and Procedures Manual repeats that "teachers are not required to be members of the Ontario College of Teachers (OCT)." — [Manual PDF](https://files.ontario.ca/edu-private-schools-policy-procedures-manual-2013-en-2021-11-25.pdf) **[CONFIRM]** whether the inspection framework imposes additional qualification expectations for credit courses (the manual's Section 6 was truncated).
- **[VERIFIED]** Ontario board examples of credential pathways: HPEDSB LSP → Certificate of Accomplishment (non-credit); HPEDSB CIP → OSSC (14 credits + LLI reading + work experience). — [Bayside SS](https://bss.hpedsb.on.ca/about_bss/special_education). (school-reported) — [Brighton School](https://brightonschool.ca/?p=70)
- **[VERIFIED]** CICE admission (Conestoga, Sept 2027 intake): "Ontario Secondary School Diploma (OSSD) or Ontario Secondary School Certificate (OSSC), or equivalent" **or** "19 years of age or older"; documented academic needs requiring curriculum modification; able to work without constant supervision; able to get to college and placements independently; interview. Certificate of Accomplishment not mentioned. — [Conestoga CICE admissions](https://www.conestogac.on.ca/fulltime/community-integration-through-co-operative-education/admissions). Georgian College: OSSD or OSSC or at least 19 on first day; 2-year Ontario College Certificate; three field placements; learning facilitators modify integrated courses. — [Georgian CICE](https://cat.georgiancollege.ca/programs/cice)
- **[VERIFIED]** Loyalist College (Belleville) CICE application package: résumé, personal goals letter (explaining "why you need a modified program"), copy of high school IEP, high school transcript, psycho-educational assessment if available, two non-family references, mandatory information session, OCAS application, interview. Admission requirements text and tuition not on the page retrieved. — [Loyalist CICE](https://loyalistcollege.com/?p=6009) **[CONFIRM]** Loyalist's exact academic prerequisite (likely the same OSSD/OSSC-or-19+ rule as other colleges).
- **[VERIFIED]** ODSP income support eligibility: 18+, Ontario resident, financial need; disability test or membership of a "prescribed class" including people already found eligible under SIPDDA 2008 (i.e., DSO-eligible), who skip the Disability Determination Package; the page "does not mention education credentials as an eligibility factor" (updated 10 Mar 2026). — [Ontario.ca ODSP eligibility](https://www.ontario.ca/page/ontario-disability-support-program-eligibility-income-support)

### Inferences
- **[PROPOSAL]** Two secondary tracks fit the population: (a) a **non-credit Certificate of Accomplishment track** built on K-coded alternative courses for most DD students, which a private school can deliver without credit-granting authority because alternative expectations are non-credit by definition; and (b) an **OSSC track** (14 credits, modified expectations with principal credit decisions, Learning Strategies, co-op) for MID students who can profit from modified curriculum. Track (b) requires the school to hold credit-granting authority (inspected private secondary) or to partner with a credit-granting body (→ legal stream).
- **[PROPOSAL]** Educational implications of *not* granting credits at opening: no loss of access to DSO, Passport or ODSP (none is credential-based); CICE remains accessible at 19+ without a credential; Project SEARCH and most supported-employment pathways target non-credit students. The real cost of a no-credit opening is to MID students who could have earned an OSSC — families of those students may prefer the public CIP. A phased plan (open with the CoA track, add OSSC credits once inspected) is consistent with how board programs separate LSP and CIP.
- **[PROPOSAL]** Because the CoA "must be accompanied by the Ontario Student Transcript," a private school issuing one should maintain OSR/transcript-equivalent records and an alternative report card (WRDSB model) so the document has meaning for colleges and employers. **[CONFIRM]** whether a non-inspected private school may issue a Ministry Certificate of Accomplishment at all (→ legal stream); if not, the school would issue its own completion certificate plus a transcript and IEP, as Storefront School does with its "Storefront Certificate."
- **[ESTIMATE]** The modified-expectations credit decision resting with "the principal" means a small school's principal must be competent in Ontario assessment policy; this is an argument for at least one OCT-certified leader with special education qualifications even where OCT membership is not legally required.

- Loyalist College CICE's formal academic admission requirement and tuition were not on the retrieved page.

## 6. Transition to adulthood at 18–21 and what the secondary program should prepare students for

### Takeaway
Ontario youth with developmental disabilities register with DSO at 16, apply for ODSP at 17.5, move to adult Passport funding at 18 ($5,665 minimum to $45,604 maximum per year, 2026 figures), and may stay in school until June of the year they turn 21, after which the main destinations are fee-for-service day programs (often funded with Passport), long waitlists for Ministry-funded day/residential supports, supported employment (ODSP Employment Supports, Project SEARCH, Employment Ontario), CICE college programs, or family care; the research describes this as "the cliff," with only ~15.5% of Canadian youth with ID aged 15–24 employed (2006 data) and ~30% of adults with DD/autism employed. A secondary program should therefore produce documented functional skills, work experience, travel/independence skills, a current psychological assessment and a DSO-ready portfolio by 18.

### Cited Findings
- **[VERIFIED]** Transition plans are required for every student with an IEP (PPM 156, issued 1 Feb 2013, effective 2 Sept 2014) and must include goals, support needs, actions, responsible persons/agencies and timelines; reviewed with the IEP; O. Reg. 181/98 requires them for exceptional students 14+. — [PPM 156](https://www.ontario.ca/document/education-ontario-policy-and-program-direction/policyprogram-memorandum-156); [Ontario.ca Transition section](https://www.ontario.ca/document/special-education-ontario-policy-and-resource-guide-kindergarten-grade-12/transition)
- **[VERIFIED]** Integrated (tri-ministry) transition planning for young people with a developmental disability begins at age 14, produces a single integrated plan coordinated with the IEP, involving principals, educators, families and community service/health providers, with regional protocols. — [Ontario.ca Integrated transition planning](https://www.ontario.ca/document/special-education-ontario-policy-and-resource-guide-kindergarten-grade-12/integrated-tri)
- **[VERIFIED]** Practical timeline (Surrey Place Community Family Guide 2024): register with DSO at 16 (requires psychoeducational/developmental assessment, IEP, ID; "process is long"); DSO eligibility letter serves as ODSP proof; apply for ODSP at 17.5; children's funding (SSAH, ACSD, OAP) ends at 18 and shifts to Passport; school attendance available until June of the year the student turns 21; Ministry-funded day-program spots have "very long waits ... years"; day programs are structured group programs (recreation, life skills, pre-employment) with staff ratios 1:5 to 1:16 and 1:1 at extra cost (up to $210/day for a support worker), usually paid fee-for-service with Passport/ODSP/private funds; Monsignor Fraser "Transition to Work" (18–20) and Project SEARCH Toronto (final year) listed as options. — [Surrey Place guide 2024](https://www.surreyplace.ca/media/Community-Family-Guide-2024.docx.pdf)
- **[VERIFIED]** Passport amounts: minimum $5,665/yr; maximum up to $45,604/yr via additional application, depending on assessed need and availability; eligible uses include community classes/recreation, work/volunteer/daily-life skills development, hiring a support worker, person-directed planning, caregiver respite (page updated 10 Sep 2026). — [Ontario.ca Passport](https://www.ontario.ca/page/passport-program-adults-developmental-disability). The April 2023 guidelines listed $5,500–$44,275 and allow pre-employment supports, job coaching, person-directed planning up to $2,500/yr, and "educational courses not eligible for OSAP" under community participation. — [MCCSS Passport Guidelines April 2023](https://files.ontario.ca/mccss-passport-guidelines-april-2023-en-2023-03-06.pdf)
- **[VERIFIED]** Kohai Educational Centre (Toronto) now operates "Kohai LIFE," a day/evening program for adults 18+ with developmental disabilities (work placements, functional academics, fitness, cooking, TTC travel), eligible for Passport funding; the organization cites "over 45 years" working with special-needs individuals; no children's school is described. — [Toronto Central Healthline](https://torontocentralhealthline.ca/displayService.aspx?id=216978); [Streets of Toronto](https://streetsoftoronto.com/school/kohai-educational-centre/)
- **[VERIFIED]** McKay (2019, *J on Developmental Disabilities* 24(1)), Hamilton-Niagara protocol study (14 professionals): children's services end at 18 and youth restart with DSO; post-secondary schooling ends at 21 ("the cliff," Mercer Delta 2006); lack of program availability cited by 78% as a major barrier; long waitlists for day programs, group homes and supported employment; youth rarely present at transition meetings; families often want youth "busy and safe"; Crawford (2011) 2006 data: 15.5% of Canadian youth 15–24 with ID employed vs 49.8% other disabilities and 58.1% no disability; recommendations: invite DSO to a transition meeting before 18, start before Grade 9, build natural community supports, educate families on adult-service realities. — [OADD JoDD PDF](https://oadd.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/41026-JoDD-24-1-v11f-27-42-McKay.pdf)
- **[VERIFIED]** Project SEARCH: 19 Ontario sites in 2024-25 (launched 2019–2024; Toronto, Halton, Hamilton, Sudbury, Guelph, Niagara, Durham, Waterloo regions); for final-year students, >70% with DD/autism not earning credits; Canada 2022-23 outcomes 67.7% employed in any job, 52.1% meeting full criteria (≥16 h/wk, competitive, year-round, ≥minimum wage), average $18.02/h and ~21 h/wk, versus ~29.9% employment among Canadian adults with DD/autism; US RCT 73.6% vs 17% control; skills-trainer cost ≈ $16,000/student; recommendation to use enhanced Passport or DSO-waitlist prioritization to fund it. — [Holland Bloorview policy brief, 28 Feb 2025](https://hollandbloorview.ca/pdf/project-search-policy-brief)
- **[VERIFIED]** Storefront School (OCDSB, 19–21): entry requires at least two prior successful independent co-op placements and independent public-transit use; 8–10 worksites over two years. — [OCDSB](https://sirguycarletonss.ocdsb.ca/download/461590)
- **[VERIFIED]** CICE requires "a level of independence that does not require constant supervision" and (Conestoga) ability to travel independently to college and placements. — [Georgian](https://cat.georgiancollege.ca/programs/cice); [Conestoga](https://www.conestogac.on.ca/fulltime/community-integration-through-co-operative-education/admissions)
- **[VERIFIED]** No Quinte-area Project SEARCH site appears in the 2024-25 list of regions named in the brief. — [same brief](https://hollandbloorview.ca/pdf/project-search-policy-brief)

### Inferences
- **[PROPOSAL]** The secondary program's "exit specification" should be reverse-engineered from the adult gates: (1) a current psychological assessment (needed for DSO and Passport) — the school should budget or broker re-assessment by age 16; (2) a DSO application started at 16 with the school supplying the IEP and transition plan; (3) ODSP application at 17.5; (4) documented, graduated work experience (in-school enterprise → supported community placement → independent placement), because Storefront, CICE and Project SEARCH all screen for prior independence; (5) travel training on Belleville Transit/Quinte Access; (6) a person-directed plan that families can carry into Passport-funded supports.
- **[ESTIMATE]** The ~$16,000/student skills-trainer cost cited for Project SEARCH and the day-program ratios (1:5–1:16) indicate the cost envelope adult services expect; a school offering 1:3–4 work-experience support (Project SEARCH ratio) for its 16–21 cohort would be at the intensive end.
- **[PROPOSAL]** A Quinte-area school could position its 18–21 programming as a local bridge given the absence of a Project SEARCH site in the region and long DSO day-program waits, but should not promise placements it cannot broker; partnership with Loyalist CICE (same city) is the most natural post-21 academic pathway.

### Gaps
- No Hastings/Prince Edward-specific data on DSO waitlists, local day-program capacity or Community Living Belleville and Area services were retrieved (desk sources not found in this pass).
- Employment data for Canadian youth with ID are old (2006 census-based); no newer Ontario-specific figure was found beyond the 29.9% adult DD/autism employment cited by Holland Bloorview.

## 7. Comparable schools — Ontario, rest of Canada, US/UK — with models, size, costs, funding and outcome evidence

### Takeaway
No Ontario private school was found that is purpose-built for IDD/MID students across elementary and secondary; Ontario comparators are either autism-focused ABA/DIR schools (Oak Bridge, Monarch, Oakwood, Magnificent Minds), LD/"learning differences" schools that admit some ID students (Brighton, YMCA Academy, TEAM, Dunblaine), or public congregated programs (HPEDSB LSP/CIP, DDSB Developmental Program, Bloorview School Authority). ## 8. Opening cohort, phased growth and staff mix: what comparators suggest

### Takeaway
Comparable programs cluster around classes of 6–12 with one special-education teacher and two assistants (DDSB 6 : 1 : 2; OCDSB DD ~10 : 1 : 2.4; Summit 10–12 : ≥2 staff; Oak Bridge 10 : 1 : 2; Project SEARCH 10 : 1 : 2; Storefront 12 : 1 : 1), i.e., an adult:student ratio of roughly 1:3 to 1:4 for DD-profile groups and up to 1:6 for more independent MID groups (BC's designated-school floor is 1:6). An opening cohort of 8–16 therefore equals one to two classes and three to six instructional staff, with therapy and behaviour expertise contracted rather than employed; English free schools open at roughly half of capacity (69 of 126) and grow by key stage.

- **[VERIFIED]** Phased opening: Suffolk SLD free school opens with 69 of 126 places, growing to capacity from year 2 onward; Edmonton autism school opened with ~44 pre-enrolled in K–2 with class caps of ~8; Lakeside horticulture started with 3 students one morning a week; Giant Steps Toronto served 24 students after 25 years in a board-subsidized model; OakHaven Academy (Oakville) lists 10 students; Dunblaine 19; Monarch 25; Applewood 25; Bright Start 25. — [Suffolk](https://www.suffolk.gov.uk/asset-library/suffolk-specification-send-free-school.pdf); [CBC Edmonton](https://www.cbc.ca/1.7161879); [Excellence Gateway](https://management-ui.excellencegateway.org.uk/sites/default/files/The%20benefits%20of%20horticulture%20projects%20for%20Young%20people%20with%20SEND.pdf); [FCSS-FESC](https://fcss-fesc.ca/publications/2021/04/25/fcss-fesc-response-to-yrdsbs-decision-to-terminate-partnership-with-giant-steps); [OurKids list](https://ourkids.net/school/developmental-disability-schools)
- **[VERIFIED]** EEF: TAs can deliver structured interventions only with training and evidence-based materials; some pupils need specialist intervention from a trained professional. — [EEF](https://dera.ioe.ac.uk/37846/1/EEF_Special_Educational_Needs_in_Mainstream_Schools_Guidance_Report.pdf)
- **[VERIFIED]** Ontario public-system context: average supported-students-to-EA ratio 19:1 elementary / 61:1 secondary (2018), suggesting the intensity gap a specialized school would be filling. — [P4E 2018](https://peopleforeducation.ca/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/Special-education.pdf)

### Inferences
- **[PROPOSAL]** Opening cohort of 8–12 students in one or two mixed-age groups (an elementary band and a secondary band), each with one special-education-qualified teacher and two EAs (≈1:3–4), gives 2 teachers + 4 EAs + a teaching principal/coordinator ≈ 6–7 FTE. For 16 students, three groups (≈9–10 FTE). This matches Summit/DDSB/OCDSB/Oak Bridge ratios and stays within Reg 298-equivalent maxima (used as a benchmark even though it does not bind private schools).
- **[PROPOSAL]** Specialist access at opening should be contracted/fractional: SLP and OT consultation (e.g., 0.1–0.2 FTE each or purchased blocks), a behaviour consultant/BCBA on retainer, and a psychologist for re-assessments; nursing only if the admitted profile requires it (section 9). Summit and Renfrew employ these in-house but at 550–600+ students.
- **[ESTIMATE]** Growth trajectory from comparators: Ontario private special-needs schools without public funding typically sit at 10–100 students after 10–20 years; publicly subsidized ones reach 140–600. A plausible plan is 8–12 (year 1) → 16–20 (year 3) → 24–30 (year 5), adding one class per year only when a full class's tuition/funding is secured, mirroring the key-stage phasing used by English free schools.

### Gaps
- No comparator published its year-by-year enrolment history; "opening cohort" evidence is limited to the Suffolk specification, the Edmonton announcement, and the Lakeside pilot.
- Ontario EA and special-education teacher salary benchmarks were not researched here (→ funding stream).

## 9. Suitability framework: who the school can responsibly serve at opening, what added resources broaden the range, and who should be excluded initially

### Takeaway
Build admission on functional domains (learning profile, communication, social/behavioural regulation, personal care and medical/mobility support) rather than IPRC label; at opening, a small school with 1:3–4 staffing and contracted SLP/OT/behaviour consultation can responsibly serve students with mild-to-moderate ID (including autism or "Multiple" profiles) who communicate functionally (speech or an established AAC system), tolerate small-group instruction with adult support, have behaviour support needs manageable with a written plan and without restraint, and need no more than intermittent assistance with personal care and no invasive medical procedures. Profiles requiring nursing-delegated procedures, two-person physical support, continuous 1:1 for safety, or active crisis-level behaviour should be deferred until nursing, behaviour-analytic staffing, accessible facilities and crisis protocols exist.

### Cited Findings
- **[VERIFIED]** Ontario policy basis for need-based rather than diagnosis-based criteria: "the need of the individual student based on an individual assessment of strengths and needs." — [Ontario.ca categories](https://www.ontario.ca/document/special-education-ontario-policy-and-resource-guide-kindergarten-grade-12/categories)
- **[VERIFIED]** OHRC: "There is no set formula for accommodating people with disabilities"; "Blanket approaches to accommodation" based on labels "will not work"; duty to accommodate up to undue hardship. — [OHRC, The Opportunity to Succeed](https://www.ohrc.on.ca/sites/default/files/attachments/The_opportunity_to_succeed%3A_Achieving_barrier-free_education_for_students_with_disabilities.pdf)
- **[VERIFIED]** Bettridge School admission indicators: need for "a highly differentiated curriculum or learning environment not normally available in mainstream"; complex significant needs in communication, cognition/learning, SEMH or sensory/physical; persistent long-term needs; exclusion of pupils whose "main presenting need is social, emotional and mental health"; specialist placement only after other options tried. — [Bettridge](https://www.gloucestershire.gov.uk/media/e1jbjh53/bettridge-school-commissioning-brief-2017-2018.pdf)
- **[VERIFIED]** Stanton Vale admits pupils totally dependent for mobility and provides "close medical oversight, access to nursing and/or skilled education care officer support," physiotherapy and adapted environments — i.e., serving that range requires those resources. — [Stanton Vale](https://www.stantonvale.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Admissions-Policy-Approved-October-2017-Review-April-2020.pdf)
- **[VERIFIED]** Summit School admits ID, autism and/or behavioural/emotional disturbances with an intake committee, evaluations February–April and a waiting list; staff include behaviour technicians and nursing. — [Summit admissions](https://summit-school.com/admissions/); [Summit home](https://summit-school.com/)
- **[VERIFIED]** DDSB Developmental Program serves medically fragile students and those needing significant sensory support with 1:2 staffing (6:1:2) and OT/PT access. — [DDSB](https://media-003-ca.cdn.govstack.com/ddsb-ca/media/v2ogvtf1/developmental-program-brochure.pdf)
- **[VERIFIED]** Magnificent Minds opened a "clinical bridging classroom" (2023) for students moving from 1:1 to group settings; states that families seeking inclusion "at all costs" may not fit. — [OurKids](https://www.ourkids.net/school/magnificent-minds-north-york/569)
- **[VERIFIED]** CICE and Storefront require independence without constant supervision and independent travel — relevant for defining the top of the secondary range. — [Georgian](https://cat.georgiancollege.ca/programs/cice); [OCDSB](https://sirguycarletonss.ocdsb.ca/download/461590)
- **[VERIFIED]** Project SEARCH's largest group (>70%) is DD/autism students not earning credits — the secondary profile the school would be preparing. — [Holland Bloorview](https://hollandbloorview.ca/pdf/project-search-policy-brief)
- **[VERIFIED]** Ontario principals' partial-day exclusions are driven mainly by safety (73%) and unavailable supports (18%) — the two failure modes a small school must plan for. — [P4E](https://peopleforeducation.ca/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/Special-education.pdf)
- **[VERIFIED]** BC does not fund mild intellectual disability or learning disability supplementally while funding moderate-to-profound ID and autism — illustrating that funders elsewhere distinguish MID from DD by support intensity. — [BC Gov](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/education-training/k-12/administration/legislation-policy/independent-schools/inclusive-education-programs-and-funding-independent-schools)
- **[VERIFIED]** Research on peer relationships shows students with socio-behavioural problems or severe disabilities are least likely to have friends and most at risk of rejection in any setting (Ferreira et al. 2017, in Dell'Anna review) — relevant to cohort composition. — [Dell'Anna et al. 2020](https://iris.unica.it/bitstream/11584/301821/1/Learning%20Social%20and%20Psychological%20Outcomes%20of%20Students%20with%20Moderate%20Severe%20and%20Complex%20Disabilities%20in%20Inclusive%20Education%20A%20Systematic%20Review.pdf)

### Inferences — proposed framework
- **[PROPOSAL] Domain 1 – Learning profile.** Serve at opening: students whose IEP uses alternative expectations and/or substantially modified expectations across most subjects (DD and MID profiles), including those labelled Autism or Multiple when the intellectual profile is the dominant educational need; learning rate consistent with explicit, repeated small-group instruction. Not at opening: students whose primary need is a specific LD or giftedness with average cognition (different instructional model; also the Ministry LD definition excludes ID), or students who are profoundly multiply disabled and working at an engagement/pre-formal level (needs a pre-formal pathway and 1:1–1:2 staffing).
- **[PROPOSAL] Domain 2 – Communication.** Serve: speech, or an established AAC system the student uses functionally; emerging language with SLP consultation. Defer: students with no functional communication system who need intensive AAC development, until SLP time is secured (an SLP-led AAC program is a precondition, not a diagnosis exclusion).
- **[PROPOSAL] Domain 3 – Social/behavioural regulation.** Serve: students who can participate in a group of 4–6 with adult support and whose behaviour support needs are documented in a plan that can be implemented without physical restraint or seclusion; episodic dysregulation manageable by trained EAs. Defer: students with current high-frequency aggression/self-injury requiring two-person response, elopement needing continuous line-of-sight 1:1, or active mental-health crisis (Bettridge's SEMH exclusion is the model), until a behaviour analyst, crisis-prevention-trained staff, a low-stimulation space and an incident protocol exist.
- **[PROPOSAL] Domain 4 – Personal care and medical.** Serve: toileting with prompts/intermittent assistance, routine medication administration under written authorization, allergy/anaphylaxis and seizure plans manageable with standard training. Defer: nursing-delegated procedures (G-tube feeding, catheterization, suctioning, complex seizure rescue beyond standard protocols), full personal care requiring lifting/two-person transfers, until a nursing arrangement (e.g., Home and Community Care Support Services school health support, or contracted RN) and accessible change facilities are in place. (→ legal stream for school-health-support eligibility of private schools.)
- **[PROPOSAL] Domain 5 – Mobility/facilities.** Serve: ambulatory students and wheelchair users if the facility is fully accessible at opening; otherwise defer wheelchair users until accessibility (ramps, accessible washroom with change table, evacuation plan) is verified. Hoisting/ceiling lifts broaden the range later.
- **[PROPOSAL] Age/stage at opening.** Comparators and Ontario count data suggest the strongest demand and the clearest program model are for ages ~12–21 (secondary DD/MID with life-skills and work pathways), with an elementary group viable if 6+ students of compatible profile enrol. Admitting across the full 6–21 range with only 8–12 students risks pathways too thin to staff; the report could recommend opening with one band (likely secondary) and adding the other when a full class is enrolled.
- **[PROPOSAL] Process.** Replicate Summit's intake committee: application with current psych assessment and IEP, classroom observation/trial days, a written "needs we can and cannot meet" statement (Bettridge/Stanton Vale practice), a 90-day review (mirroring the 3-month review right under Reg 181/98), and a documented transition-out plan so no family is left without a placement if the school cannot meet a need. Cohort composition should be checked for social compatibility (age gap, communication level, behaviour mix) before each admission, because peer-relationship research shows students with severe behaviour are most likely to be rejected even in specialized groups.
- **[PROPOSAL] Resources that broaden the range later (in rough order of cost):** CPI/behaviour-safety training for all staff; contracted BCBA; SLP-led AAC program; accessible washroom/change space; part-time RN with delegation; sensory/low-arousal room; 1:1 EA capacity funded case-by-case (families' SSAH or OAP funds cannot be assumed; → funding stream); pre-formal/engagement pathway with 1:2 staffing.

### Gaps
- No Ontario private school publishes a written admissions exclusion policy comparable to the English examples; the framework above is a synthesis, not a copy of any Ontario practice.
- Whether Ontario's school health support services (nursing/therapy delivered in schools via Home and Community Care) extend to private schools was not confirmed here. **[CONFIRM]** (→ legal/funding streams)
- Local availability of SLP/OT/BCBA contractors in the Belleville area was not researched.
