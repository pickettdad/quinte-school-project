# Quinte IDD School Needs Million-Dollar Donors

**Bottom line.** A small, tuition-free, five-day independent school for students with intellectual developmental disability (IDD) and mild intellectual disability (MID) in the Belleville–Trenton–Stirling–Tyendinaga catchment is educationally designable, legally straightforward to establish, and operationally workable in an adapted church building — but it is **not financially feasible on the base case as specified** unless a transformational funder is in hand. Fully costed, with transportation, meals and premises included and no tuition, the school needs roughly **$1.0 million a year at 12 students, $1.4 million at 20 and $2.0 million at 30 (ESTIMATE, 2028-29 prices)**, of which confirmed non-philanthropic sources can cover **at most 3–5 percent**; there is no Ontario or federal operating stream for private schools **(VERIFIED)**, and every family-directed program (OAP, SSAH, Passport, ODSP) expressly excludes tuition **(VERIFIED)**. That annual philanthropic requirement — about **$1 million even at the smallest viable size** — exceeds what any Belleville-area charity raises from the community (Hospice Quinte, after 40 years, raised ~$0.88M in donations and fundraising in FY2023 **(VERIFIED, CRA T3010)**) and equals roughly 43 percent of the entire United Way Hastings & Prince Edward campaign ($2.26M in 2025 **(VERIFIED)**). The three newly surplus HPEDSB Belleville schools are not a realistic premises route on this timeline (Minister-directed disposition under O. Reg. 374/23 at fair market value, priority to boards, the Crown and housing/LTC bodies; regional precedents took 2.5–6 years and all went to public buyers), and provincial policy bars JK–12 private schools from partnering for space in operating schools **(VERIFIED)**. The lead premises candidate is a listed, accessible, CF-zoned church at 253 Dundas St W (asking $950,000; buy-and-adapt ~$1.4–2.5M versus $8–10M and 3–3.5 years for a new build **(ESTIMATE)**). A September 2028 opening is achievable only if, by roughly December 2027, the founding board holds signed multi-year pledges covering 60–70 percent of Year-1 operating cost plus an 18–24-month cash reserve — on the order of **$0.6–0.7M/yr pledged and $1.5–2.0M in reserve at 12 students, before the ~$2.1–2.8M start-up cost (PROPOSAL/ESTIMATE)**. Opening earlier than 2028 is not credible: CRA charitable registration alone takes 6–12 months, and the Notice of Intention/validation cycle requires the school to be physically operating with at least five compulsory-age pupils when the Ministry inspector arrives in September. The honest headline is: **the model works if, and only if, the money is anchored by one or two very large recurring commitments or an endowment/land gift; the demand, program and law do not stand in the way, the Belleville philanthropic market does.**

Labels used throughout: **VERIFIED** (primary or near-primary source, link and date given), **ESTIMATE** (arithmetic shown or basis stated), **PROPOSAL** (a design or planning choice, not an existing fact), **CONFIRM** (requires confirmation with the named authority before reliance). Research tooling limits that prevented reading primary sources are flagged in-line and consolidated at the end.

## 1. The educational model: pathways by need, bands by age, work by sixteen

### Group by learning profile inside two age bands

The strongest evidence about *what* works for students with ID is about instruction, not setting. A four-year IES-funded longitudinal study of 141 children with IQ 40–80 (Allor et al. 2014) delivered 40–50 minutes of daily explicit reading instruction in groups of 1–4 per teacher and found that students with mild-to-moderate ID could read independently at a first-grade level or higher, that the intervention group significantly outperformed comparison students, and that IQ did not reliably predict achievement **(VERIFIED)** ([News-Medical summary, 22 May 2014](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20140522/Four-year-educational-study-offers-hope-for-children-with-intellectual-disability.aspx)). Systematic reviews converge: phonics-based and systematic explicit instruction are effective but require "a lot of time and intensive practice" ([Alnahdi 2015 via ASHA](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/1ab638ff-61c8-4475-b834-a65e28eec7a4); [Hill 2016 via ASHA](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/d30d82f0-8436-4eb8-aca4-292df3e11354)); task-analytic instruction with systematic prompting, time delay and in-vivo instruction are evidence-based practices for academic and mathematics teaching to students with significant cognitive disabilities **(VERIFIED)** ([Spooner et al. 2011](https://intelligentlives.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/Evidence-Based-Practices-for-Teaching-Academic-Skills-to-Students-with-Severe-Developmental-Disabilities.pdf); [Hudson, Rivera & Grady 2018 via TEA](https://spedsupport.tea.texas.gov/resource-library/complex-access-needs-research-summaries/mathematics)). The Education Endowment Foundation's SEND guidance adds that teaching assistants should "supplement the classroom teacher, not replace them," can deliver structured small-group interventions only with training and evidence-based materials, and that poor deployment "can harm pupils' learning and wellbeing" **(VERIFIED)** ([EEF guidance report, 2020](https://dera.ioe.ac.uk/37846/1/EEF_Special_Educational_Needs_in_Mainstream_Schools_Guidance_Report.pdf)).

Specialized schools in England and Canada consistently place students on curriculum "pathways" by attainment and need rather than by age, while keeping chronological bands for social appropriateness: Ash Lea's four pathways are "not defined by age, but by learning needs and attainment" ([Ash Lea](https://www.ashlea.notts.sch.uk/curriculum-pathways/)); Hazel Court (ages 11–19, Ofsted Outstanding 2024) runs three curriculum routes with a bridging route to functional-skills qualifications ([Ofsted](https://files.ofsted.gov.uk/v1/file/50252955)); Summit School in Montreal groups elementary students "for academic subjects by level" and runs classes of ~10–12 with a minimum of two staff ([Summit programs](https://summit-school.com/programs/)) **(VERIFIED, school-reported for Summit)**. Locally, HPEDSB separates a non-credit secondary Life Skills Program (Certificate of Accomplishment) from a credit-bearing Community Integration Program (14-credit OSSC with Leveled Literacy Intervention and work experience) **(VERIFIED)** ([Bayside SS special education](https://bss.hpedsb.on.ca/about_bss/special_education)).

**PROPOSAL.** The workable model for 12–30 students is two or three learning-pathway groups for core literacy and numeracy (by current skill and communication level, re-assessed each term, with movement allowed), nested inside two age bands — roughly ages 6–13 and 14–21 — used for homeroom, social curriculum, personal-care privacy and community/work programming. Each band has one special-education-qualified teacher and two educational assistants (1:3–4), matching DDSB (6:1:2), OCDSB DD classes (~10:1:2.4), Oak Bridge (10:1:2) and Project SEARCH (10:1:2) **(VERIFIED ratios)** ([DDSB brochure](https://media-003-ca.cdn.govstack.com/ddsb-ca/media/v2ogvtf1/developmental-program-brochure.pdf); [OCDSB report](https://ocdsb.ca/download/483711); [Holland Bloorview Project SEARCH brief, Feb 2025](https://hollandbloorview.ca/pdf/project-search-policy-brief)). Ontario's O. Reg. 298 s.31 class maxima (DD 10; autism/multiple 6; MID 12–16) do not bind a private school but are a sensible benchmark **(VERIFIED via Ministry summary; regulation text not read — tooling limit)** ([Ontario.ca summary](https://www.ontario.ca/document/special-education-ontario-policy-and-resource-guide-kindergarten-grade-12/summary)). The literacy block should mirror Allor's design: for a 12-student cohort, three to four concurrent groups of 1–4, meaning at least three instructional adults during that block **(ESTIMATE)**. Comparators suggest opening with the band where demand and program model are clearest — ages ~12–21 — and adding the elementary band when six or more compatible students enrol, because spreading 8–12 students across ages 6–21 makes pathways too thin to staff **(PROPOSAL)**.

### Kitchen, garden, crafts and market as curriculum, not labour

Student-run cafés, coffee carts, gardens and enterprise stalls are common practice in Ontario board programs and UK special schools and are consistently run by a special-education teacher plus one or two EAs, priced to cover supplies, and tied to IEP goals rather than accreditation **(VERIFIED, descriptive case evidence only)**: TCDSB's BCN Café had students with multiple exceptionalities stocking carts, brewing, handling money and banking under a teacher, resource staff and two EAs ([Catholic Register, 1 May 2016](https://catholicregister.org/item/22235-bcn-cafe-teaches-special-needs-students-life-skills)); Durham's "Daily Drip" elementary coffee cart routed profits to an end-of-year trip ([Education News Canada, 8 Nov 2024](https://educationnewscanada.com/article/education/level/k12/3/1110555/-the-daily-drip-coffee-cart-comes-to-clara-hughes-ps.html)); Lakeside/Mudlarks horticulture began with three students one morning a week with two TAs and insisted "accreditation should not drive the project" ([Excellence Gateway, June 2013](https://management-ui.excellencegateway.org.uk/sites/default/files/The%20benefits%20of%20horticulture%20projects%20for%20Young%20people%20with%20SEND.pdf)); Oakfield Park gives work-experience students a dedicated job coach and 1:1 "when necessary" ([Oakfield Park](https://www.oakfieldpark.wakefield.sch.uk/post16/teaching-and-learning)). The only numeric supervision guidance found is UK CLEAPSS P183 (Feb 2024): no more than 10 children using sharp tools at once, no more than 3 heating food under "very close supervision," an extra briefed adult recommended **(VERIFIED)** ([CLEAPSS P183](https://primary.cleapss.org.uk/Resource-File/P183-Cooking-in-a-classroom.pdf)). No Ontario source specifies ratios for cooking with students with ID.

**PROPOSAL.** Supervision benchmarks: kitchen sessions at no more than 1 adult to 3 students when heat or knives are in use; garden and craft at about 1:4–5 with two adults minimum; café or market service at 1:3–4 with one adult on cash reconciliation and one on food hygiene. Every task is tied to an IEP alternative-expectation goal with recorded progress; roles rotate so no student becomes a fixed "worker"; there is a termly opt-out and choice of role; and student labour never subsidizes operations (section 6 budgets the market at net zero). The teaching kitchen is treated as a food premises from day one — see section 8.

### Mentoring and cross-age contact without caregiving

Peer tutoring has high-quality evidence (EEF: +6 months average progress across 145 studies; pupils with SEN make some of the biggest gains; cross-age tutoring works best with an age gap under three years, structured tasks and trained tutors) **(VERIFIED)** ([EEF Toolkit](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/peer-tutoring)). No published policy was found on preventing older students with ID from being cast as caregivers in special schools. **PROPOSAL.** Cross-age contact should be reciprocal, time-limited, trained peer tutoring or shared activities (clubs, market, garden) with a ≤3-year age gap where tutoring occurs; written policy should state that students are never responsible for another student's safety, toileting, feeding, medication or behaviour, and that adult:student ratios are calculated without counting older students. Adult community mentors (volunteers with vulnerable-sector checks; see section 8) and workplace mentors at community placements fill the mentoring role for teens.

### Teen-appropriate programming and the 18–21 bridge

The secondary day should look different from the elementary day: work-related learning, travel and independence training, personal finance, relationships and health, and graduated community placements. The OCDSB Storefront School (ages 19–21, max 12, one teacher plus one job coach) runs five mornings a week of 2.5-hour placements across 8–10 worksites over two years ([OCDSB Storefront](https://sirguycarletonss.ocdsb.ca/download/461590)); Halton's Community Pathways Program graduates all students with a Certificate of Accomplishment plus optional Employability or Community Skills Certificates ([HDSB](https://cks.hdsb.ca/ck-programs/special-education/community-pathways-program)); Summit's Work-Oriented Training Path starts at 15 **(VERIFIED)**. Project SEARCH — 19 Ontario sites in 2024-25, none in the Quinte area — reports 67.7 percent of Canadian graduates employed (52.1 percent meeting full criteria) against ~29.9 percent employment among Canadian adults with DD/autism, with a skills-trainer cost of about $16,000 per student **(VERIFIED)** ([Holland Bloorview, Feb 2025](https://hollandbloorview.ca/pdf/project-search-policy-brief)). Only 15.5 percent of Canadian youth 15–24 with ID were employed in 2006 data, and transition professionals describe the end of school at 21 as "the cliff" **(VERIFIED)** ([McKay 2019, JoDD](https://oadd.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/41026-JoDD-24-1-v11f-27-42-McKay.pdf)).

### Secondary pathways and transitions

Ontario's credentials are the OSSD (30 credits), the OSSC (14 credits, on request at 18) and the Certificate of Accomplishment (no credit threshold, for students leaving at 18 without OSSD/OSSC, accompanied by the Ontario Student Transcript with the IEP optionally attached) **(VERIFIED)** ([OS K–12 PPR 2024, ch. 6, updated 14 Jan 2026](https://www.ontario.ca/document/ontario-schools-kindergarten-grade-12-policy-and-program-requirements/diploma-and-certificate-requirements-related-procedures)). Alternative expectations are by definition outside the Ontario curriculum, carry K-codes and "are not eligible for course credit"; whether modified expectations earn credit is "the principal's decision" **(VERIFIED)** ([PPR 2024 ch. 3](https://www.ontario.ca/document/ontario-schools-kindergarten-grade-12-policy-and-program-requirements/meeting-needs); [ch. 7](https://www.ontario.ca/document/ontario-schools-kindergarten-grade-12-policy-and-program-requirements/secondary-school-courses-and-related-procedures)). **PROPOSAL.** Two tracks: a non-credit Certificate-of-Accomplishment track built on K-coded alternative courses, which a non-inspected private school can deliver; and an OSSC track for MID students who can profit from modified curriculum, which requires credit-granting authority through Ministry inspection (section 8) — phased in later, if at all. Not granting credits at opening closes no adult door: DSO, Passport and ODSP eligibility are not credential-based, and college CICE programs admit on "OSSD or OSSC or 19 years of age or older" **(VERIFIED)** ([Conestoga CICE](https://www.conestogac.on.ca/fulltime/community-integration-through-co-operative-education/admissions); [Georgian CICE](https://cat.georgiancollege.ca/programs/cice); [Ontario.ca ODSP eligibility, 10 Mar 2026](https://www.ontario.ca/page/ontario-disability-support-program-eligibility-income-support); [Passport, 10 Sep 2026](https://www.ontario.ca/page/passport-program-adults-developmental-disability)). Loyalist College in Belleville runs a Ministry-approved CICE program (since Sept 2017) and asks applicants for an IEP, transcript and psycho-educational assessment ([Loyalist CICE](https://loyalistcollege.com/?p=6009)); its formal academic prerequisite was not on the page retrieved **(CONFIRM with Loyalist College)**. The real cost of a no-credit opening falls on MID students who could have earned an OSSC; their families may prefer the public CIP.

The exit specification for the secondary program should be reverse-engineered from the adult gates **(PROPOSAL, built on VERIFIED timeline)**: a current psychological assessment by 16 (needed for DSO and Passport); DSO registration at 16 with the school supplying the IEP and PPM 156 transition plan; ODSP application at 17.5; graduated work experience from in-school enterprise to supported to independent placements (Storefront, CICE and Project SEARCH all screen for prior independence); travel training on Belleville Transit and Quinte Access/Transit; and a person-directed plan families can carry into Passport-funded supports ($5,665 minimum to $45,604 maximum per year) ([Surrey Place guide 2024](https://www.surreyplace.ca/media/Community-Family-Guide-2024.docx.pdf); [PPM 156](https://www.ontario.ca/document/education-ontario-policy-and-program-direction/policyprogram-memorandum-156)). Students may remain in school until the last school day in June of the year they turn 21 **(VERIFIED, Education Act s.33(1) as quoted by a board policy; statute not read — tooling limit)** ([Avon Maitland AP 302](https://www.amdsb.ca/apps/pages/ap302)).

## 2. Who the school could serve: HPEDSB counts overstate, prevalence confirms a pool of a few hundred

### Suitability criteria built on need, not label

Ontario's five statutory categories use functional definitions: a student with MID has "an ability to profit educationally within a regular class with the aid of considerable curriculum modification," whereas a student with a Developmental Disability has "an inability to profit from a special education program for students with mild intellectual disabilities because of slow intellectual development" and "limited potential for academic learning, independent social adjustment, and economic self-support"; the Ministry's principle is that "the determining factor … is not any specific diagnosed or undiagnosed medical condition, but rather the need of the individual student" **(VERIFIED)** ([Ontario.ca categories, updated 19 Mar 2026](https://www.ontario.ca/document/special-education-ontario-policy-and-resource-guide-kindergarten-grade-12/categories)). The Learning Disability definition expressly excludes students with intellectual disabilities. **PROPOSAL.** Admission should be decided on five domains, with written "needs we can and cannot meet" statements (the Bettridge and Stanton Vale practice), an intake committee with trial days (Summit's practice), a 90-day review and a documented transition-out plan: (1) learning profile — IEPs using alternative or substantially modified expectations across most subjects, including Autism or Multiple labels where the intellectual profile is the dominant educational need; (2) communication — speech or an established AAC system used functionally; (3) regulation — able to participate in a group of 4–6 with adult support under a behaviour plan implementable without restraint or seclusion; (4) personal care and medical — toileting with prompts, routine medication under written authorization, standard anaphylaxis and seizure plans; (5) mobility — ambulatory, or wheelchair users only if the facility is fully accessible. Deferred until resources exist: students needing nursing-delegated procedures, two-person physical support, continuous 1:1 for safety, intensive AAC development without an SLP program, or in active mental-health crisis. Ontario principals report that partial-day exclusions are driven mainly by safety (73 percent) and unavailable supports (18 percent) — the two failure modes the school must plan against **(VERIFIED)** ([People for Education 2018](https://peopleforeducation.ca/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/Special-education.pdf)).

### What the HPEDSB exceptionality data shows — and does not

The identical table for 31 October 2024 appears in HPEDSB's 27 February 2025 SEAC agenda and is **VERIFIED**: IPRC-identified elementary/secondary counts of IDD 122/171 (293), MID 41/40 (81), Autism 363/198 (561), Multiple 116/183 (299), Learning Disability 213/337 (550), total 1,039/1,101 (2,140); IEPs 3,093 among 15,513 students (19.9 percent), of which 1,591 (51.4 percent) carry "No Exceptionality" ([HPEDSB SEAC agenda, 27 Feb 2025](https://www.hpedsb.on.ca/common/pages/GetFile.ashx?key=sktYARW1)). The 31 October 2025 figures the project holds (IDD 118/187 = 305; IMD 31/56 = 87; Autism 432/223 = 655; Multiple 124/187 = 311; LD 200/344 = 544) were not located in any fetchable document; they are internally consistent with the 2024 table (changes of +6 to +94 per cell) and most likely come from the 26 February 2026 SEAC agenda or the 2026–27 Special Education Plan Appendix C(a) **(CONFIRM with HPEDSB Student Services)**. The only cell worth querying is autism (+17 percent in one year).

Three cautions govern how these figures may be used. First, the table carries this footnote, quoted exactly: "For students with an exceptionality of Multiple, the data also reflects the Exceptionality 2 and Exceptionality 3 information in the totals below (new)" **(VERIFIED)**. The columns are therefore **not mutually exclusive**; each Multiple student may be counted two or three times across columns. The arithmetic bears this out: the column sum of 2,140 exceeds the 1,502 students with an exceptionality on an IEP (3,093 − 1,591) by about 640, consistent with the footnote **(ESTIMATE)**. Board counts must never be presented as unique students. Second, HPEDSB identifies intellectual exceptionalities at roughly 2.5 times the provincial rate — IDD+MID 374 ÷ 15,513 = 2.41 percent (Oct 2024) versus Ontario's 19,085 ÷ 2,095,193 = 0.91 percent (2024-25 OnSIS) **(ESTIMATE from VERIFIED inputs)** ([Ontario Data Catalogue, 2024-25](https://data.ontario.ca/dataset/b498b7ab-c6d0-4791-b8bd-c25c58e5bdbf/resource/d644aa6c-371d-48ba-aaed-e6c560abfc72/download/special_education_enrolment_by_exceptionality_2024-2025_en.txt); [Ontario Education Facts](https://www.ontario.ca/page/education-facts)). The board figure is therefore an upper-bound indicator, not a conservative one; no "hidden unidentified" uplift should be added. Third, the secondary IDD count (171–187) exceeds the elementary count (118–122) even though secondary enrolment is roughly a third of the board, which almost certainly reflects accumulation of identification with age **and** students remaining in LSP/CIP classes to age 21 **(ESTIMATE)**. Nothing in the fetched documents states the age cut-off; the number of 18–21-year-olds inside the secondary IDD/MID columns is unknown **(CONFIRM with HPEDSB)**. This matters for the school's Notice of Intention scope (adult students are permitted — section 8) and for Passport-funded programming (section 5).

Prevalence triangulation supports the same conclusion. The core catchment (Belleville 55,071; Quinte West 46,560; Stirling-Rawdon 5,015; Tyendinaga Township 4,538; Tyendinaga Mohawk Territory ~2,100–2,200 on-territory; Deseronto 1,747 — 2021 census, **VERIFIED via Wikipedia mirrors; StatCan pages errored — tooling limit**) holds about 115,000 people, 67 percent of the HPEDSB territory, with an estimated 21,400 residents aged 4–21 **(ESTIMATE)**. At the generally accepted ~1 percent prevalence of intellectual disability (Maulik 2011; H-CARDD Ontario adult DD prevalence 0.78 percent) that implies ~215 children with ID in the core and ~300 board-wide **(ESTIMATE)** ([McKenzie et al. 2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40474-016-0085-7); [Lunsky et al. 2014](https://www.longwoods.com/content/24026/primary-care-of-adults-with-developmental-disabilities-in-ontario)) — below the board's identified IDD+MID count, confirming that the board count already captures essentially the whole epidemiological population plus double-counting.

### The demand funnel

Building from a primary pool of about **480–630 families** across the HPEDSB territory (IDD+MID 374–392, a net 40–100 Multiple students with an intellectual component not already counted, 45–120 ALCDSB students in the catchment at provincial-to-HPEDSB identification rates on ~4,865 enrolled, and 5–20 from Quinte Mohawk School, French and private schools), applying geographic reach (section 3), fit with a small school at opening (60–80 percent) and family propensity (15–30 percent would seriously consider; 5–12 percent would enrol at opening), the interest pool is **about 15–60 families under a 30-minute ride, 25–100 under 45 minutes and 35–130 under 60 minutes; the opening cohort absent further evidence is high single digits to high thirties at 45 minutes (ESTIMATE; every step assumption-driven, most sensitive to propensity, which no desk source pins down)**. ALCDSB's documented Life Skills sites are in Kingston, so Belleville/Trenton Catholic-board families may be a more receptive segment ([Holy Cross Life Skills](https://www.alcdsb.on.ca/School/hcss/Programs/lifeskills/Pages/default.aspx)). Competition is chiefly the board's own 21 elementary resource programs and 26 LSP/CIP classes — a rough 350–450 seats, comparable to the whole identified pool **(VERIFIED locations; capacity ESTIMATE)** ([HPEDSB Elementary Resource Program Summary 2025-26](https://cdnsm5-ss11.sharpschool.com/UserFiles/Servers/Server_8476374/File/Programs%20&%20Learning/Special%20Education/Elementary%20Resource%20Program%20Summary%202025-2026%20April%2015_2025.pdf); [Secondary Regional Program Summary 2025-26](https://cdnsm5-ss11.sharpschool.com/UserFiles/Servers/Server_8476374/File/Programs%20&%20Learning/Special%20Education/Secondary%20Regional%20Program%20Summary%202025-2026%20April%2015_2025.pdf)). **PROPOSAL.** Decision-grade evidence requires an anonymous 8–12-question survey distributed through intermediaries that already reach these families (Community Living Belleville & Area, Community Living Quinte West's Family Engagement Network and Transitional Age Youth program, the Children's Treatment Centre at Quinte Health, SEAC member associations), targeting 80–130 completes (±7.5–10 points at 95 percent confidence against N≈550), plus a separate opt-in expression-of-interest list; ≥40–60 EOIs inside the 45-minute zone with ≥25 "likely/very likely" supports planning a 20–30-student opening; fewer than 25 signals a Belleville-only or later start. Boards cannot release family data under MFIPPA, and Indigenous engagement on Tyendinaga Mohawk Territory should follow the Mohawks of the Bay of Quinte's own approval protocols.

## 3. Location, catchment and transportation: 45 minutes is the workable compromise

A Belleville hub on the Highway 2/62 corridor or near the 401/62 interchange minimises satellite-run times; the Belleville–Trenton corridor already hosts 60–65 percent of HPEDSB's congregated seats **(ESTIMATE from program locations)**. Direct drive times from Belleville are Trenton 19 min, Frankford 23, Stirling 22, Deseronto 31, Picton 31, Tweed 34, Madoc 36, Napanee 28 **(VERIFIED via Rome2Rio; Google Maps blocked — treat as ±10 percent)** ([Rome2Rio Belleville–Trenton](https://www.rome2rio.com/s/Belleville-ON-Canada/Trenton-ON-Canada)). School-bus one-way time is roughly 1.2 × direct drive time plus 2–3 minutes per stop (3–5 minutes for lift or harness loading) **(ESTIMATE)**.

|---|---|---|---|
| **30 min** | Belleville urban, Cannifton, Corbyville, Foxboro, Point Anne, Bayside, Rossmore, Shannonville; Trenton only as a single express stop | ~35–40% | Door-to-door feasible |

| **60 min** | + Tweed, Madoc/Ivanhoe, Picton/Bloomfield/Wellington, Napanee, Brighton edge; Marmora and Bancroft out at any cap | ~75–85% | Longest, costliest, most fatigue-prone rides for students with IDD |

All figures **ESTIMATE**. Tri-Board Student Transportation's 2026 policy targets ≤75 minutes one way for JK–8 and ≤90 for 9–12, transports "only enrolled students" of its member boards within attendance boundaries, and says nothing about private schools or at-cost service **(VERIFIED)** ([Tri-Board Student Transportation Policy 2026](https://triboard.ca/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Student-Transportation-Policy-2026.pdf); [Tri-Board eligibility, 6 Aug 2026](https://triboard.ca/eligibility/)). Transportation is therefore a school operating cost line, not a public service the school can assume. Quinte Transit's specialised door-to-door service explicitly contemplates "shared rides for education" but only on the Quinte West/PEC side ([Quinte Transit listing](https://east.behaviouralsupportsontario.ca/Services/Display/73681/Quinte_Transit)).

The public-system benchmark is ~$1,965 per transported student (HPEDSB 2026-27: $20.21M for 10,285 students, 23,740 daily km, about $4.39/km) **(VERIFIED inputs; division is ESTIMATE)** ([HPEDSB 2026-27 Final Budget Book](https://cdnsm5-ss11.sharpschool.com/UserFiles/Servers/Server_8476374/File/2026-2027%20Final%20Budget%20Book.pdf)) — a floor built on 70-passenger buses at high load, unreachable for a tiny dedicated accessible service. Modelled costs **(ESTIMATE; no Ontario rate card obtained — CONFIRM with Sharp, Switzer-CARTY and Voyago quotes)**: contracted small accessible buses at ~$440/bus/day cost $256,000 at 12 students under a 30-minute cap (three buses; $21,300/student), $170,700 at 45–60 minutes (two buses; $14,200/student); owned used lift buses at ~$46,500–51,000/bus/yr cost $102,000 at 12 students under 60 minutes ($8,500/student plus $70–90K capital); a hybrid (one owned bus for the Belleville core plus contracted service for the satellite hubs, 45-minute cap) costs **$136,400 at 12, $187,400 at 20 and $238,400 at 30 students ($11,400 / $9,400 / $7,900 per student)**. The hybrid 45-minute model is adopted as the base case in section 9. Driver licence classes are **VERIFIED** (Class E for school-purposes buses up to 24 passengers; Class B unlimited; Class G for cars and vans) ([MTO Driver's Handbook, 14 Sep 2026](https://www.ontario.ca/document/official-mto-drivers-handbook/getting-your-drivers-licence)); a bus seating 10 or more needs a CVOR ([Ontario.ca CVOR, 9 Feb 2026](https://www.ontario.ca/page/commercial-vehicle-operators-registration-cvor)); whether a 12-passenger van carrying students is a "school purposes bus" turns on HTA s.175 and O. Reg. 612, neither of which could be read **(CONFIRM with MTO — tooling limit)**.

## 4. Premises: the surplus schools are a mirage on this timeline; a church is the realistic path

### Why the three surplus HPEDSB properties are unlikely to be available by 2028

HPEDSB declared three Belleville properties surplus effective 28 September 2026 — Queen Victoria School (46 Pine St), Quinte Secondary School (45 College St W) and Sir Winston Churchill PS (301 MacDonald Ave) — under O. Reg. 374/23 s.2 and "has engaged the Ministry of Education to start disposition. ## 5. The no-cost-to-families model: a $1 million-a-year philanthropic gap at the smallest size

### What the school cannot access

Ontario "provides no funding or financial support to private schools" **(VERIFIED)** ([Ontario.ca Private schools, updated 2 Oct 2026](https://www.ontario.ca/page/private-schools)). The board-partnership streams were restructured for 2025-26: ECPP is now confined to youth-justice and specified provincial facilities, and the new Care and Treatment Education Programs (CTEP) stream covers students who "cannot attend school because of their primary need for care and/or treatment," with the partner facility expected to be funded by MCCSS or the Ministry of Health and to provide daily treatment **(VERIFIED)** ([CTEP Directives 2025-26](https://efis.fma.csc.gov.on.ca/faab/Section_23/25-26/EN/edu-2526-ctep-directives-en-2025-06-24.pdf); [ECPP Directives 2025-26](https://efis.fma.csc.gov.on.ca/faab/Section_23/25-26/EN/edu-2526-ecpp-directives-en-2025-06-24.pdf)); the 2024-25 guideline states that special-education needs "should be accommodated through programs and services offered through the school board" ([ECPP Guidelines 2024-25](https://efis.fma.csc.gov.on.ca/faab/Section_23/24-25/EN/edu-2425-ecpp-guidelines-en-2024-04-26.pdf)). A community organisation serving students who *can* attend school does not fit CTEP, and a CTEP partnership would hand the board control of admission, staffing and records and exclude students aged 21+. No Education Act mechanism was found permitting a board to contract instruction of its pupils to a third party **(statute not readable — CONFIRM with board counsel)**. Every family program is closed to tuition: SSAH lists "private school tuition for the child" as ineligible ([SSAH guidelines, 1 Apr 2026](https://www.ontario.ca/document/special-services-home-program-guidelines)); OAP core clinical services exclude "private school tuition fees, tutoring" ([OAP guidelines, 10 Sep 2026](https://www.ontario.ca/page/ontario-autism-program-guidelines-core-clinical-services-and-supports)); Passport excludes OSAP-eligible tuition and therapies ([Passport Guidelines, April 2023](https://files.ontario.ca/mccss-passport-guidelines-april-2023-en-2023-03-06.pdf)) **(all VERIFIED)**. ODSP ($1,436/month single maximum from July 2026), the Canada Disability Benefit ($204.20/month), the Child Disability Benefit ($3,480/yr), ACSD ($25–678/month), the Disability Tax Credit and RDSP are income supports or tax measures to the family and are **$0 school revenue**; the medical expense tax credit for "care and training" fees would matter only if the school charged fees, which it will not ([CRA Folio S1-F1-C1, 15 Aug 2025](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/technical-information/income-tax/income-tax-folios-index/series-1-individuals/folio-1-health-medical/income-tax-folio-s1-f1-c1-medical-expense-tax-credit.html)).

### What the school can access — small, late and mostly one-off

Ontario Trillium Foundation Seed ($10–100K), Capital ($10–200K, requires ownership or a lease with at least five years remaining) and Grow ($50–200K/yr for programs with "a proven track record") all require 12 months of incorporated financial history and two years of statements for Grow **(VERIFIED)** ([OTF Grow](https://otf.ca/our-grants/community-investments-grants/grow-grant); [OTF Capital](https://otf.ca/our-grants/community-investments-grants/capital-grant); [OTF 2026–2029 Business Plan](https://otf.ca/document/1155)); whether a school is inside OTF's four sectors could not be confirmed (eligibility page robots-blocked) **(CONFIRM with OTF)**. The first realistic OTF application is therefore 2028 and Grow not before 2029–30; OTF is $0 in the opening budget. The City of Belleville's grant streams cap Community Impact at $10,000 (cash plus in-kind) and Social Infrastructure at $20,000, exclude school boards (private schools not mentioned), require $5M liability insurance, and state that grants "should not be treated as a recurring source" **(VERIFIED)** ([Belleville Grant Policy, Oct 2025](https://www.belleville.ca/en/city-hall/resources/Documents/Community-Funding-and-Grants/Grant-Policy_October-2025.pdf)). Canada Summer Jobs reimburses up to 100 percent of minimum wage for not-for-profits — summer wages only, ~$10K ([CSJ Applicant Guide, 29 Sep 2026](https://www.canada.ca/en/employment-social-development/services/funding/canada-summer-jobs/applicant-guide.html)). The federal Enabling Accessibility Fund is capital-only, its 2026 call is closed, and the 2026 round required applicants to already employ a person with a disability ([EAF 2026 who can apply](https://www.canada.ca/en/employment-social-development/services/funding/enabling-accessibility-fund-contribution/who-can-apply.html)). United Way HPE requires audited or reviewed statements and a "sustainable, stable and strong" organisation — realistically a November 2029 intake at the earliest, for programs rather than core operations **(ESTIMATE)** ([InQuinte, 14 Oct 2025](https://www.inquinte.ca/?p=1336)). MCCSS transfer-payment status for a new agency is speculative; no open intake exists. Two in-kind streams are real: Ontario Health atHome School Health Support Services are expressly available to students "in a publicly funded school, private school, or home-school program," including nursing, dietitian and — because Children's Treatment Centres cover only public schools — PT, OT and SLP, on the principal's referral **(VERIFIED, fact sheet dated 04-2026)** ([OHaH SHSS fact sheet](https://ontariohealthathome.ca/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/OHaH-School-Health-Support-Services-Fact-Sheet-EN.pdf)) **(CONFIRM scope with OHaH South East)**; and the Student Nutrition Program is "open to all children and youth at each program site," delivered locally by the Hastings and Prince Edward Learning Foundation, with private-school site eligibility unstated **(CONFIRM with HPELF)** ([Ontario SNP, 11 Jun 2026](https://www.ontario.ca/page/student-nutrition-program)). Both are avoided costs, not revenue.

### What families' programs may legitimately purchase

The only lawful route for family funding to reach the school is as a purchased service the program allows and the school is qualified to deliver: SSAH-funded before/after-school care, PA-day, March-break and summer respite and "social skills programs" for under-18s (the guidelines are silent on school-hours respite — **CONFIRM with the MCCSS Special Agreements Officer**); OAP childhood-budget respite and skill-building, and OAP-funded ABA/SLP/OT only when delivered or supervised by college-registered professionals; and, for students 18–21, Passport-funded life-skills, pre-employment and community-participation programming — Passport explicitly covers adults "still in school," with a $5,665 floor and a $45,604 ceiling, purchasable from "adult education providers" or via a ministry-funded Transfer Payment Recipient **(VERIFIED)**. Modelling one program per student and only non-instructional services, this yields roughly **$1,000–1,500 per student per year on average, i.e., $20–30K at 20 students (ESTIMATE; plausible, not committed)**.

### Corporate sponsorship, naming, in-kind and bus advertising

Corporate money comes in two legally distinct forms that must be kept on separate T3010 lines: receiptable donations (line 4500; eligible amount reduced by any advantage; no receipt if the advantage exceeds 80 percent; de minimis advantages up to the lesser of $75 or 10 percent) and sponsorships (line 4630; promotional value received, not receiptable, deductible to the business as advertising) **(VERIFIED)** ([CRA split receipting, 22 May 2018](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/charities-giving/charities/operating-a-registered-charity/issuing-receipts/split-receipting.html); [CRA CG-013](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/charities-giving/charities/policies-guidance/fundraising-registered-charities-guidance.html)). No Ontario statute binds independent schools' naming policies; the constraints are CRA and the school's own board-approved donor-recognition and naming policy with revocation clauses **(VERIFIED by absence; PROPOSAL)**. Realistic local tiers once mature: one or two presenting-level anchors at $25–50K/yr, 5–10 mid-sized firms at $5–10K, 20–30 small businesses at $1–2.5K — roughly $100–200K/yr at maturity, one-third of that in year one, counted only on signed multi-year pledges with a 10–20 percent non-fulfilment discount **(ESTIMATE)**. Donated services cannot be receipted; gifts-in-kind of property can be receipted at fair market value and appear as a capped reduction of the matching expense line, never as cash. A "sponsored bus" is a restricted gift, not new revenue, and must not be counted in addition to corporate giving. Bus advertising is immaterial: the last Ontario consortium deal (1997) returned 50 percent of gross to boards and operators; US district figures imply $1–2K per bus per year gross; 1–3 buses would net perhaps **$1,000–5,000 a year (ESTIMATE)**, and O. Reg. 612's marking rules could not be read **(CONFIRM — tooling limit)** ([Strategy, 8 Dec 1997](https://strategyonline.ca/?p=9313)).

## 6. Student market and enterprise: curriculum first, $0–5,000 net at best

The Belleville Farmers' Market (169 Front St; Tuesday, Thursday, Saturday year-round) charges daily vendors $30 per stall plus a $10 association fee, or $200 per stall per year with parking plus $80; it invites community groups and charities to identify themselves, and prepared-food vendors need public-health approval via a Food Vendor Notification **(VERIFIED)** ([Belleville Farmers' Market, Become a Vendor](https://www.bellevillefarmersmarket.ca/become-a-vendor)). A weekly Saturday stall over 20 market weeks costs ~$800 in fees (or ~$280 on an annual lease) plus $1,500–3,000 in materials; at $150–300 gross per market day, gross sales are $3,000–6,000 and **net after fees and materials is about $500–3,000 before staff time**; adding two on-site market days and a staff coffee cart brings realistic net to **$0–5,000 a year (ESTIMATE)**. The EA time to run it (20 × 5 h × $26 ≈ $2,600) is the real cost. CRA's related-business rules (CPS-019) are satisfied because the enterprise is an off-shoot of a charitable program, subordinate to it and largely volunteer-run ([CRA CPS-019](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/charities-giving/charities/policies-guidance/policy-statement-019-what-a-related-business.html)). The budget books the market at net zero: it is curriculum (money handling, customer service, production planning, teamwork) with face validity and IEP alignment, not a revenue line, and must never be structured so that student labour subsidizes operations **(PROPOSAL)**.

## 7. Financing: no interest-free institution exists; borrowing is a recurring fundraising obligation

No source offers a brand-new charity with no revenue an interest-free institutional loan. The federal Social Finance Fund is repayable, market-return-seeking capital routed through intermediaries; ONN's January 2025 briefing says nonprofits "can't apply directly" and that "the current structure of the SFF is not set up to address these barriers" for small early-stage organisations, with no Ontario-specific intermediary named **(VERIFIED)** ([ONN SFF Briefing Note, Jan 2025](https://theonn.ca/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/SFF-Briefing-Note-External-JAN-2025.docx-1.pdf); [ESDC SFF](https://www.canada.ca/en/employment-social-development/programs/social-innovation-social-finance/social-finance-fund.html)). Community Forward Fund lends to nonprofits but publishes no rates or terms ([Community Forward Fund](https://communityforwardfund.ca/)); community bonds presuppose an asset, a revenue stream and an engaged investor base ([Tapestry, 8 Sep 2026](https://tapestrycapital.ca/community-bonds/)); Infrastructure Ontario's loan program serves listed public-sector clients, and private schools or charities do not appear to be among them **(CONFIRM — site not readable)**. Credit unions bridge confirmed grant receivables at prime-plus and often ask directors of new organisations for personal guarantees.

**PROPOSAL.** Interest-free money is therefore philanthropic debt: a donor or foundation loan agreement (principal, 0 percent, term, repayment schedule, subordination, forgiveness triggers tied to milestones such as charitable registration or enrolment), with the lender able to forgive and be receipted for the forgiven principal as a gift at that time **(CONFIRM CRA treatment of forgiven loans with charity counsel)**; a forgivable loan; or a donor guarantee of a credit-union line (e.g., $250K) so the school can bridge grant holdbacks and pledge timing without any director signing personally. Bridging is a finance expense (prime + 1–3 percent on the drawn amount for 60–90 days: hundreds to low thousands of dollars), not a funding source. The affordability arithmetic is unforgiving: **$500,000 interest-free over 10 years is $50,000 a year of principal that donations must generate (≈5 percent of the 12-student budget); the same loan at 5 percent costs ≈$63,600 a year; $250,000 at 0 percent over 5 years is $50,000 a year; a $712,500 mortgage on Property A at 6 percent over 25 years is ≈$54,700 a year (ESTIMATE)**. Every loan converts a one-time capital need into a recurring fundraising obligation for the term and is added to the operating requirement. Borrowing is shown in a separate sources-of-capital schedule, never in the revenue table.

**Clear flag on personal guarantees.** ONCA directors are not generally personally liable for corporate debts, with exceptions such as unpaid employee wages, and have a due-diligence defence **(VERIFIED)** ([ONCA Guide, updated 15 Oct 2024](https://www.ontario.ca/page/guide-not-profit-corporations-act-2010)). A personal guarantee of a lease, mortgage or operating line strips that protection. The founding board should adopt a written policy that no director, officer or employee gives a personal guarantee for any obligation of the corporation, and should decline any lender or landlord that requires one; a donor guarantee or a larger reserve is the substitute **(PROPOSAL)**.

## 8. Legal structure and operations: incorporation is days, CRA is months, validation is unforgiving

### Structure and registration

Incorporation under the Not-for-Profit Corporations Act, 2010 is as of right through the Ontario Business Registry for $155 with a five-business-day online standard; a charitable corporation is automatically a public benefit corporation — minimum three directors, no more than one-third employees, review engagement mandatory above $100K revenue and audit mandatory at $500K and above **(VERIFIED)** ([Ontario.ca fees, 29 Sep 2026](https://ontario.ca/page/cost-time-required-to-register-change-search-for-business-name-corporation-not-for-profit); [ONCA Guide](https://www.ontario.ca/page/guide-not-profit-corporations-act-2010)). Registered charities may not pay directors without a court order **(commentary; CONFIRM O. Reg. 4/01 with counsel)** ([LawyerInfo.ca, 24 Jun 2026](https://lawyerinfo.ca/guides/ontario/business-law-ontario/business-formation-ontario/how-to-incorporate-a-not-for-profit-corporation-under-the-onca-in-ontario/)), so founders who intend to be paid staff should be employees reporting to a majority-independent board, not voting directors. Operating a school is an established "advancement of education" charitable purpose; restricting beneficiaries by disability is an expressly recognised permissible restriction (CRA's example: "a school for the hearing impaired limited to students with hearing impairment"); and the absence of fees raises no issue, since education purposes carry no poverty requirement **(VERIFIED)** ([CRA CG-030, 27 Nov 2020](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/charities-giving/charities/policies-guidance/advancement-education-charitable-registration.html)). CRA's published 2025-26 standard is a final decision within nine months, met about 80 percent of the time; applications go online via My Business Account since the fax line was retired 1 April 2026 **(commentary; CONFIRM on canada.ca)** ([Charity Law Group, updated 27 Aug 2026](https://www.charitylawgroup.ca/charity-law-questions/charity-registration-timeline-canada-what-to-expect); [Miller Thomson, 20 Mar 2026](https://www.millerthomson.com/en/insights/social-impact/update-from-the-canada-revenue-agency-for-charities-retiring-the-fax-line-effective-april-1-2026/)). The risks CRA will probe are private benefit (above-market founder salaries, founder-controlled boards), related-business status of the enterprise, and direction and control over funds; because education charities no longer face a political-activity cap but may never support a candidate or party ([CRA CG-027](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/charities-giving/charities/policies-guidance/public-policy-dialogue-development-activities.html)), the charity's resources cannot be used in any trustee campaign.

### Private-school status: Notice of Intention, validation, non-inspected route

Ontario "does not regulate, license, or accredit private schools" and "does not inspect or approve premises, health and safety practices, or staffing"; the only Ministry gate is the annual Notice of Intention due 1 September (one-time $300 fee for new schools) followed by an unannounced validation visit, usually within the first 30 days, confirming the school is operating 9 a.m.–4 p.m. on school days for five or more pupils of or over compulsory school age with a principal in charge and a central records office; success yields a BSID, failure means the school must stop operating for the rest of that school year **(VERIFIED)** ([Ontario.ca NOI page, 29 May 2024](https://ontario.ca/page/private-schools-notice-intention-operate-private-school); [Private Schools Policy and Procedures Manual, 2013 ed.](https://files.ontario.ca/edu-private-schools-policy-procedures-manual-2013-en-2021-11-25.pdf); [NOI form 52-0147E](https://forms.mgcs.gov.on.ca/dataset/4b1662b4-a7a4-49dd-9ae7-773e86fa40aa/resource/05e988ac-a51b-4f28-b4b7-dd3722a851d8/download/52-0147e.pdf)). Operating without an NOI carries a $50-per-day fine; the Auditor General found Ontario has "one of the least regulated private school sectors in Canada" ([AG 2013, s.3.06](https://www.auditor.on.ca/en/content/annualreports/arreports/en13/306en13.pdf)). Adult students (18+) are expressly contemplated by the Manual, and no source prohibits mixed-age enrolment, but the five-pupil floor counts only pupils aged 6+ on validation day **(CONFIRM wording of Education Act s.21(1) — statute not readable)**. A school that is not yet operating on validation day fails; the NOI must not be filed for a year in which opening is uncertain.

Credit-granting authority comes only from a fee-based Ministry inspection under s.16(7) (last published fee $2,450 in 2012-13 rising to a planned $4,050; current fee unpublished — **CONFIRM with the Private Schools and International Education Unit**) and requires OSSD-structured course calendars, 110-hour courses, Growing Success assessment and OSR management ([Denial and Revocation, Sept 2023](https://www.ontario.ca/files/2023-09/edu-denial-revocation-authority-grant-credits-2022-2023-en-2023-09-19.pdf); [Ontario.ca Private schools](https://www.ontario.ca/page/private-schools)). For a cohort whose programming is largely K-coded alternative expectations, the non-inspected route is appropriate at opening. Because the OSSC is credit-based, a non-inspected school cannot issue it; the Certificate of Accomplishment is non-credit but is framed as granted by the Minister on the principal's recommendation with Ministry forms reserved to inspected schools, so the safest reading is that a non-inspected school issues its own leaving certificate with a transcript-equivalent and IEP **(CONFIRM with the Ministry)**. The 2013 Manual's Section 6 on special education could not be read **(tooling limit)**.

### Staffing qualifications and employment law

"Principals and teachers at private schools do not have to be certified by the Ontario College of Teachers"; criminal background checks are not legally required for private-school staff **(VERIFIED)** ([Ontario.ca Private schools](https://www.ontario.ca/page/private-schools); [OCT Council memo, 14 Feb 2020](https://solstice.oct.ca/about-the-college/council/-/media/E1BEE26F137E4115A7EBEBA247CE44D9.ashx); [AG 2013](https://www.auditor.on.ca/en/content/annualreports/arreports/en13/306en13.pdf)). **PROPOSAL.** Adopt as policy, though not required: OCT certification with Special Education AQs for teachers and the principal (the modified-expectations credit decision rests with the principal, which argues for one OCT-certified leader even on the non-credit route); vulnerable-sector checks through the local police service for all staff and volunteers (only police can issue them) ([Ontario.ca police record checks, 27 Apr 2026](https://www.ontario.ca/page/police-record-checks)); first aid/CPR and non-violent crisis-intervention training. Legally binding regardless: the ESA, OHSA (including a written violence and harassment program), the Human Rights Code duty to accommodate, AODA policies and training (no compliance report below 20 employees), the CYFSA duty to report, and OCT Act s.43.2 reporting if any OCT member is employed **(VERIFIED)** ([AODA rules, 16 Jun 2026](https://www.ontario.ca/page/accessibility-rules-businesses-and-non-profits); [Report child abuse, 20 Apr 2026](https://www.ontario.ca/page/report-child-abuse-and-neglect)). WSIB's 2026 Educational Services rate is $0.33 per $100 of payroll; whether private schools are mandatory (Schedule 1 Part I) or coverage-by-application (Part II) could not be confirmed because O. Reg. 175/98 was unreadable **(CONFIRM with WSIB — tooling limit)** ([WSIB 2026 premium rates](https://www.wsib.ca/en/2026-premium-rates)). Registered charities claim the $1M Employer Health Tax exemption ([Ontario EHT exemption, 22 May 2026](https://www.ontario.ca/document/employer-health-tax-eht/tax-exemption)).

### Safeguarding and health policies to adopt voluntarily

Sabrina's Law (anaphylaxis), Ryan's Law (asthma), PPM 161 (prevalent medical conditions), PPM 158 (concussion), PPM 144 (bullying) and PPM 128 (code of conduct) are framed as duties of school boards and do not on their face bind private schools **(VERIFIED)** ([OS K–12 s.1, updated 4 Sep 2026](https://www.ontario.ca/document/ontario-schools-kindergarten-grade-12-policy-and-program-requirements/learning-environment)); courts and insurers will nonetheless hold the school to the standard of a reasonably prudent school. **PROPOSAL.** Adopt board-equivalent policies for individual plans of care, medication administration, concussion, safe arrival, anti-bullying, restraint/seclusion (with staff training), a two-adult rule, privacy (PHIPA where health information is held), whistleblower/complaints, conflict of interest, gift acceptance and financial controls. The Immunization of School Pupils Act's application to private pupils could not be read from the statute **(CONFIRM with Southeast Public Health — tooling limit)**; the public-facing page says all children attending primary or secondary school must be immunized unless exempt ([Ontario.ca vaccines at school, 16 Sep 2025](https://www.ontario.ca/page/vaccines-children-school)). The local health unit now operates as Southeast Public Health ([Southeast Public Health food safety, 7 Oct 2026](https://southeastph.ca/health-topics/food-and-healthy-eating/food-safety/)).

### Food premises, child-care exemption, transportation and insurance

A teaching kitchen preparing meals is a food premises under O. Reg. 493/17: notification to the health unit before opening, inspection, a dedicated hand-wash sink, commercial dishwashing and at least one certified food handler on site during operation, with low-risk pre-packaged programs exempt from some requirements **(VERIFIED via health-unit summaries; regulation text not read — CONFIRM classification with Southeast Public Health)** ([York Region, Food Safety in Schools](https://www.york.ca/health/food-safety/food-safety-schools); [SMDHU](https://www.simcoemuskokahealth.org/docs/default-source/jfy-schools/safehealthyfoodatschool2019webversion.pdf?sfvrsn=8)). The Child Care and Early Years Act "does not apply to private schools that only care for children ages 4 years old and older" **(VERIFIED)** ([Ontario.ca child care rules, 28 Oct 2025](https://www.ontario.ca/page/child-care-rules-ontario)), so before/after-school care for enrolled students aged 4+ stays exempt on the plain wording **(CONFIRM with the Ministry's child-care licensing branch)**. Contracting a licensed school-bus operator shifts CVOR, O. Reg. 612 and driver-licensing compliance to the contractor, leaving the school with non-owned-auto insurance and supervision duties. Insurance is unregulated beyond the Manual's requirement that community-involvement participants be "adequately covered by the school's insurance"; advisable coverages are commercial general liability with an abuse/molestation extension, directors' and officers' liability, property, non-owned and (if applicable) owned auto, student accident and tenant's liability, at a planning cost of $15–22K/yr **(ESTIMATE; CONFIRM with a broker)**.

### Governance

Minimum legal governance is ONCA's; the recognised voluntary benchmark is Imagine Canada's Standards Program (board governance, financial accountability, fundraising, staff and volunteer management), open to organisations of any size ([Imagine Canada Standards](https://www.imaginecanada.ca/en/standards-program)). **PROPOSAL.** A founding board of at least five, majority independent of any future employee, with finance/audit, program and development committees; a written admissions policy documenting disability-and-need-based selection consistent with CG-030 paras 58–60; T3010 within six months of year-end (mandatory online from 2027) ([Carters, 25 Jun 2026](https://www.carters.ca/?p=15984)).

### Comparable schools and the setting evidence, even-handedly

No Ontario private school purpose-built for IDD/MID across elementary and secondary was found. Ontario comparators are autism-focused ABA/DIR schools (Oak Bridge, Cambridge: ~100 students, $32–47K tuition, 10:1:2; Monarch, 25 students, $30–32.5K, 1:3; Magnificent Minds, 50 students, $26–60K, 1:4), LD-focused schools admitting some ID students (Brighton, ~70 students, $25–30.5K, offers OSSD/OSSC/CoA; Dunblaine, a charity, 19 students, $27.3K), and public congregated programs **(VERIFIED, school-reported via OurKids)** ([OurKids developmental-disability list](https://ourkids.net/school/developmental-disability-schools)). Belleville's Applewood Academy is a therapeutic boarding model at $30,750 day tuition, not an IDD day school ([DORIS profile](https://www.doris.school/schools/canada/applewood-academy-for-progressive-learning)). Every long-lived full-scope model is publicly subsidized: Summit School (Montreal, 600+ students, ages 4–21, tuition-free, "recognized and subsidized in the public interest," classes of 10–12 with two staff, in-house psychology, SLP, OT and nursing) ([Summit admissions](https://summit-school.com/admissions/)); Renfrew (Calgary, 550 students, $1,000–4,500 tuition, Alberta-funded); Janus Academy (Calgary, founded by five families in 1996, 95 students at $17,140 with Alberta's 70 percent per-student rate) ([OurKids Janus](https://www.ourkids.net/school/janus-academy/1315)); BC's designated special-education schools (1:6 ratio, 50 percent of the public rate plus $2,000, and "no new schools are currently being designated") ([BC Gov](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/education-training/k-12/administration/legislation-policy/independent-schools/inclusive-education-programs-and-funding-independent-schools)); English special free schools (£10,000 per place plus £10,500–16,000 top-ups) ([Suffolk specification](https://www.suffolk.gov.uk/asset-library/suffolk-specification-send-free-school.pdf)). Giant Steps Toronto, which ran ~25 years on a York Region DSB partnership at $7,000 a year to families, lost that partnership in 2021, affecting 24 students — a warning about dependence on a single board relationship ([Global News, 13 May 2021](https://globalnews.ca/news/7855741/school-children-with-autism/amp)). Ontario comparators without public funding plateau at 10–100 students after 10–20 years; subsidized ones reach 140–600 **(ESTIMATE)**. No independent outcome evaluation exists for any private comparator.

The evidence on specialized versus inclusive settings does not support a claim that congregated settings produce better outcomes, nor the opposite. The 2022 Campbell systematic review (94 non-randomised studies, 15 meta-analysed) found small, non-significant effects favouring inclusion — psychosocial adjustment SMD 0.20, literacy 0.04, math 0.05 — and concluded it is "very unlikely that inclusion in general increases or decreases learning and psychosocial adjustment," with low-quality evidence throughout **(VERIFIED)** ([Dalgaard et al. 2022, DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/b90de3a9e36f4ce38d609eedb0dc0275)). Individual studies cut both ways: Dessemontet (2012) found slightly more literacy progress for included children with ID and no difference in math or adaptive behaviour ([summary](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/school-classroom/effects-of-inclusion-on-the-academic-achievement-and-adaptive-behaviou/)); Buckley (2006) found mainstream teens with Down syndrome 2.5–3.3 years ahead in expressive and written language but special-school teens scoring higher on interpersonal relationships ([DSE library](https://www.down-syndrome.org/en-us/library/research-practice/09/3/comparison-mainstream-special-education-teenagers-down-syndrome-implications-parents-teachers)); Olsson (2018, via Dell'Anna 2020) found more friends in inclusive schools (47 vs 31 percent) but higher happiness in special settings (62 vs 33 percent), and Matzen found instructional time of 85–96 percent in inclusive versus 48–56 percent in self-contained classes ([Dell'Anna et al. 2020](https://iris.unica.it/bitstream/11584/301821/1/Learning%20Social%20and%20Psychological%20Outcomes%20of%20Students%20with%20Moderate%20Severe%20and%20Complex%20Disabilities%20in%20Inclusive%20Education%20A%20Systematic%20Review.pdf)); Heiman (2000) found special-school adolescents with mild ID had fewer friends and felt lonelier ([Open University record](https://cris.openu.ac.il/en/publications/friendship-quality-among-children-in-three-educational-settings/)); Fuchs, Gilmour and Wanzek (2025) conclude fifty years of placement research is "fundamentally flawed" by selection bias and that many students need intensive specialized instruction in a separate setting, while critics note omitted studies showing post-school benefits of inclusion ([Hechinger Report, 2025](https://hechingerreport.org/proof-points-special-education-inclusion-research-flawed/)). Canadian authority is positional: the Supreme Court in *Eaton* held integration "can be either a benefit or a burden" and the test is the child's best interests ([OHRC consultation paper](https://www.ohrc.on.ca/en/consultation-paper-education-and-disability-human-rights-issues-ontarios-education-system/iii-human)); the OHRC, Inclusion Canada and the Canadian Down Syndrome Society favour regular-class placement while CDSS "respects the right of families to make personal decisions" ([CDSS position](https://Www.Cdss.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/Inclusive-Education-Position-Statement.pdf)). The school's case should therefore rest on instructional intensity, continuity and belonging, and it must design against the documented risks of a congregated setting — smaller friendship pools, downtime, loss of typical-peer language models — through structured peer tutoring, shared community activities and maximised instructional minutes **(PROPOSAL)**.

## 9. Financial feasibility: the verdict and the conditions

### Reconciling the two per-student cost bases

The education stream triangulated **$35,000–45,000 per student** from three sources: English SLD place-plus-top-up funding (£20,500–23,000 ≈ CAD $35–40K), Ontario private tuition for comparable intensity ($25–47K, school-reported) and an OCDSB autism class costed at ~$250,000 for six students (~$42K). The premises/staffing stream produced **$66,000–84,000 per student**. The difference is not a disagreement about the price of teaching; it is a difference in scope and scale. The $35–45K figures are marginal classroom costs at scale: English top-ups fund 100+-pupil schools whose transport is paid separately by the local authority; private tuition excludes transport and meals and comes from lean, often for-profit, 25–100-student schools; the OCDSB figure counts 1.19 teacher and 2.0 EA FTE only, with the board carrying principal, premises, transport, clinicians and administration. The fully costed stream counts everything a stand-alone free school must pay: at 12 students, staffing alone is $52,600 per student because two teachers and four EAs serve 12 students and the principal, administrator, cook, custodian and $115K of contracted SLP/OT/BCBA/psychology time are largely fixed; transport adds $11,400; premises, meals, insurance, supplies, administration, fundraising, reserve and contingency add the rest. The two bases converge where they should: the fully costed staffing-only line falls to **$43,800 per student at 30 students**, inside the education stream's range. **This report adopts the fully costed basis** ($1.01M / $1.44M / $1.97M; $84K / $72K / $66K per student) because it alone answers the question actually asked — the full cost of a free school that also covers transport, meals and activities — and uses the education stream's figure only as the check that the staffing model is not inflated **(ESTIMATE)**.

### Confirmed versus speculative revenue, cash flow, reserves and borrowing

Confirmed revenue at opening: **$0**. Plausible but uncommitted: family purchased services $12–45K, City of Belleville ≤$20–30K (non-recurring), CSJ ~$10K, enterprise $0–5K. In-kind (OHaH therapy and nursing, SNP food if a site is approved, donated professional services) appears as capped avoided cost. Tax credits (donation credit, DTC, METC) appear nowhere as revenue. Loan proceeds appear in a capital schedule; repayments appear as expense. Donations (line 4500) and sponsorship (line 4630) are separate lines so the CRA fundraising ratio can be computed. **No tuition appears anywhere.**

**PROPOSAL — committed recurring funding required before opening (go/no-go gate):** signed multi-year pledges (three or more years) covering at least 60–70 percent of Year-1 cash need, plus an 18–24-month operating reserve in cash or irrevocable pledges, before any lease is signed or staff hired. At 12 students that is **≈ $0.6–0.7M/yr pledged plus ≈ $1.5–2.0M in reserve, on top of ≈ $2.1–2.8M of start-up (of which ≈ $1.3–1.7M could be mortgage-financed, ideally with a donor guarantee)**; at 20 students, **≈ $0.85–1.0M/yr pledged plus ≈ $2.2–2.9M reserve**. The rationale: no government operating stream exists; OTF and United Way eligibility begins only after one or two audited years, so Years 1–2 are 100 percent donor-dependent; CRA expects a justified reserve policy (CG-013 Appendix C); and a school cannot ethically close mid-year on students who cannot easily be moved. Sector practice cites 3–6 months as a *minimum* reserve for established charities; 18–24 months is this report's proposal for a start-up with no earned revenue **(PROPOSAL)**.

### Sensitivities and major dependencies

Transport contracted at a 30-minute cap instead of hybrid adds $188K at 30 students (+$6,300/student); owned buses at 60 minutes save $34K. Teachers at public-board loaded cost add $111K (12) to $222K (30); a third EA per class adds $91K to $182K; dropping group benefits saves $30–60K. Leasing 10,000 sq ft instead of owning adds ~$155K/yr but avoids $1.3–1.7M of capital. Losing the property-tax exemption adds $12–18K. Fully donated food saves ~$25K. Fundraising at a realistic 20–25 percent cost-of-raising adds $200–475K to the gross raise across the scenarios. Enrolment lag is the sharpest: at 12 students the fixed core is ≈ $230K, so every student below plan adds ~$1,600 to the others' average cost, and the five-pupil validation floor must be met on day one **(all ESTIMATE)**. The major dependencies are, in order: (1) one or two anchor funders or an endowment/land gift; (2) decision-grade family interest (≥40–60 EOIs in the 45-minute zone); (3) an accessible, correctly zoned building closed by early 2028; (4) CRA registration by early 2028 so pledges are receiptable; (5) a principal hired a year before opening; (6) a contracted or owned accessible transport solution; (7) Southeast Public Health, Belleville Building/Fire and OHaH South East confirmations.

### Milestones to a September 2028 opening (working back from 1 September 2028)

**September 2028 — open and be validated:** the school must be operating by the first week of September with at least five compulsory-age pupils, a principal in charge and a central records office, because the Ministry's unannounced validation visit comes within roughly 30 days and failure means closure for the year **(VERIFIED)**. NOI and $300 fee submitted on or before 1 September 2028 through the new-school OnSIS account requested in spring 2028. **June–August 2028:** staff onboarding, vulnerable-sector checks (allow several weeks), OHSA/ESA/AODA set-up, fire safety plan approved, food-premises notification filed, OHaH referrals initiated, transport contract or vehicles in place, trial days for admitted students. **February–July 2028:** fit-out and change-of-use permit work (12–18 months total from purchase, so purchase must close by about January 2028 at the latest); hire teachers and EAs by spring. **December 2027 — funding gate (go/no-go #2):** pledges and reserve at the levels above in hand; CRA registration expected late 2027 to early 2028 (application filed early 2027); if the gate is not met, **do not file an NOI for 2028** and re-plan for September 2029. **September 2027:** pre-opening principal/executive director hired (12 months before opening, per the start-up budget); conditional offer on premises with zoning pre-consultation, building-condition and designated-substances surveys. **February–June 2027 — demand gate (go/no-go #1):** interest survey and EOI list run February–April when families receive placement decisions; decision rule applied by June. **January–March 2027:** ONCA incorporation ($155, days), bylaws and core policies, founding board of five or more, CRA application filed online. **Now to December 2026:** founding board formed, case for support drafted, prospective anchor funders identified (not approached without authorization). Opening earlier than September 2028 is not credible: the CRA, premises and funding gates cannot be compressed, and a September 2027 opening would require a building closed and a principal hired within months of incorporation with no receipted fundraising.

### Conditions of viability — the headline verdict

**The proposal is viable only if** a transformational funding anchor exists: realistically one or two donors or a foundation committing on the order of **$0.6–1.0M a year for at least five years**, or an endowment/land gift of several million dollars, with the balance raised from a mature local program of major gifts, monthly donors, corporate sponsorship and events that itself takes three to five years to build. Under that condition, everything else in this report — the pathway model, the staffing ratios, the adapted church, the hybrid transport, the non-inspected Certificate-of-Accomplishment route, OHaH-funded therapy — is achievable for September 2028 and would deliver what the question asks for: excellent education and belonging at no cost to families. **The proposal is not viable** on ordinary community philanthropy: the smallest viable school needs roughly as much community giving every year as Hospice Quinte raises after forty years, and the comparators that have lasted (Summit, Renfrew, Janus, English free schools) all rest on public per-student subsidy that Ontario does not provide and that BC and Alberta ration or have closed. Nor is the gap closed by family programs (3–4 percent), grants (small, late, one-off), enterprise ($0–5K) or bus advertising ($1–5K). The honest conclusion is that the **educational and legal feasibility is high, the financial feasibility is low without an anchor, and the September 2028 date should be treated as conditional on the December 2027 funding gate rather than as a commitment.**

## Conclusion

The research reframes the question. The hard problems a founding board might expect — regulation, credentials, teacher certification, premises law, demand — are either light (Ontario barely regulates private schools; OCT membership is not required; incorporation takes days) or tractable (a listed accessible church, a hub-and-spoke 45-minute transport design, a non-credit pathway that closes no adult door, OHaH-funded therapy that private-school students can access in ways public-school students cannot). The problem that is not tractable by design or diligence is that a tuition-free stand-alone school in a market of this size must raise, every year and indefinitely, more than any existing local charity raises from the community, with no government backstop and no path to one. Every comparable that has survived at scale is publicly subsidized; every Ontario comparable without subsidy either charges $25–47K or stays tiny.

That points to two implications for whoever authorizes the next steps. First, the sequence should be inverted: test the funding anchor before testing demand or touring buildings, because without it the other work is wasted; the December 2027 gate is the real decision, and a candid case for support should say so. Second, if the anchor is not found, the same evidence supports a smaller, fundable starting point that builds the track record OTF, United Way and donors require — for example a Passport-fundable 18–21 transition and community-participation program, or an after-school and PA-day respite program billable to SSAH, operating from a church hall on weekdays — rather than a full five-day school that cannot clear the five-pupil, 30-day validation test and then must close. The region's identified population is real, the model is sound, and the law is permissive; the money, not the mission, decides.
