# Admissions criteria, intake process and accommodation obligations

**Status:** Phase 2 research, 2026-10-15. Desk research only; no authority contacted. Builds on v1 note `educational_design_and_comparables.md` §9 (suitability framework) and v1 report §2. The criteria below are **PROPOSAL** for the founding board; the legal statements are labelled individually. Companion file: `research/educational-design.md` (what the opening school can staff; what added resources broaden the range).

Labels: **VERIFIED** (primary source read, link and access date), **SECONDARY**, **ESTIMATE**, **PROPOSAL**, **ASSUMPTION**, **FOUNDER DECISION**, **CONFIRM**.

---

## 1. Principles (PROPOSAL, consistent with the brief)

1. Admission is decided on **the individual student's learning, communication, social/regulation and support needs** and on whether the school, with the resources it actually has, can meet them — never on diagnosis alone (Ministry principle: "the need of the individual student based on an individual assessment of strengths and needs", v1 §1 VERIFIED).
2. **Predicted economic productivity, employability, "potential" or perceived personal worth are never admission criteria** (FOUNDER DECISION, brief). Nor are IQ cut-offs, family income or donor status, a family's capacity to volunteer, religion, race, ancestry, citizenship, family status, or any other Human Rights Code ground beyond the disability-and-need basis of the program itself.
3. The school is a program for students with intellectual and developmental learning needs. Restricting enrolment to that group is lawful as a **special program** under Human Rights Code s.14 (programs "designed to … help disadvantaged people or groups to achieve, or try to achieve, equal opportunity"; organizations "do not need permission from the OHRC"; the OHRC guide's own example is a service that serves only people with disabilities — VERIFIED, OHRC *Your guide to special programs and the Human Rights Code*, approved March 2010, updated December 2013, accessed 2026-10-15) and consistent with CRA guidance that selection by disability and need is a permitted restriction for a charitable school (v1 legal note, CG-030 paras 58–59). **CONFIRM** with charity counsel that the admissions policy wording matches the charitable purpose.
4. Every decision is **individualized, documented, explained in writing, and reviewable**. "No" is always "not at this time, for these reasons, and here is what would change it."
5. Students with **Down syndrome** are assessed through exactly the same criteria and process. There is no separate pathway, quota or presumption in either direction (FOUNDER DECISION). The same applies to any other syndrome or diagnosis: the diagnosis tells the intake committee what to look for, not what to decide.

## 2. Admission criteria — the opening population

The school serves students aged 4–21 (to the last school day in June of the year the student turns 21, mirroring Education Act s.33(1), v1 §1 VERIFIED via board policy) whose needs sit inside the five domains below *at the time of intake*. The domains are from v1 §9 (PROPOSAL), tightened here into criteria an intake committee can apply and a family can read.

| Domain | The school can serve at opening (criterion) | Needs the school cannot yet meet — what would change it (see `educational-design.md` §8) |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Learning profile | Learning needs that require alternative and/or substantially modified expectations across most subjects — IDD, MID, and autism or "Multiple" profiles where the intellectual/developmental profile is the dominant educational need; learns through explicit, repeated, small-group instruction. Evidence: current IEP (if any), psychological or developmental assessment (any age), school reports. A formal IPRC identification is **not** required (the OHRC duty applies "whether or not the student has gone through a formal IPRC process, or has an IEP" — VERIFIED, OHRC 2018 policy §2.2.1). | Primary need is a specific learning disability or giftedness with average cognition (a different instructional model; the Ministry's LD definition excludes ID — v1 §1 VERIFIED). Pre-formal/engagement-level learners — until a pre-formal pathway with 1:2 staffing exists. |
| 2. Communication | Speech, or an established AAC system the student uses functionally, or emerging language that the contracted SLP (0.4 FTE) can program for within the communication block. | No functional communication system and a need for intensive AAC development — until SLP capacity of about 0.6 FTE-equivalent and a device program exist. |
| 3. Social and behavioural regulation | Can take part in a group of 4–6 with adult support; any behaviour support needs are documented in a plan that staff can implement without physical restraint or seclusion; episodic dysregulation manageable by trained EAs and the 0.2 FTE behaviour consultant. | Current high-frequency aggression or self-injury requiring two-person response; elopement needing continuous line-of-sight 1:1; active mental-health crisis — until a ≥0.5 FTE BCBA, two trained responders per shift, a low-arousal room and an incident protocol exist. |
| 4. Personal care and medical | Toileting with prompts or intermittent assistance (continence is **not** a criterion); routine medication under written authorization; anaphylaxis, asthma, diabetes and seizure plans manageable with standard training. | Nursing-delegated procedures (G-tube, catheterization, suctioning, complex seizure rescue); full personal care needing lifting or two-person transfers — until an RN delegation arrangement, trained staff, a medical room and an adult change facility exist (OQ-3 on Ontario Health atHome). |
| 5. Mobility and facilities | Ambulatory students; wheelchair users where the premises are fully accessible on the day of admission (accessible route, washroom with change table, evacuation plan). | Hoisting/ceiling-lift transfers — until equipment and two-person training exist. |

Two further **group-viability tests** apply to every offer, because the school is a set of small groups, not a set of seats:
- **Compatible homeroom test:** there is a homeroom whose age band fits the student (bands: 4–7, 8–10, 11–13, 14–16, 17–18, 19–21) and whose current composition — communication levels, behaviour-support mix, pathway spread — can absorb the student without pushing any adult:student ratio past the staffing rules in `educational-design.md` §5.6. Peer-relationship research shows students with the most severe behaviour are the most likely to be rejected even inside specialized groups (v1 §9, VERIFIED), so the committee looks at the *group* the student would join, not only the student.
- **Age-respect test:** no student is placed in a homeroom outside their chronological band to make a group work.

## 3. Intake process (PROPOSAL)

Timeline is for a September start; rolling intake follows the same steps on a shorter clock when a place is open.

| Step | When | What happens | Record created |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Information session | October–January | Open sessions (in person and online; accessible formats on request — AODA) explaining program, criteria, what the school cannot yet provide, transport, the no-tuition model and the OSSC/credit position. No pre-screening at this step. | Attendance list (contact only) |
| 2. Application | By 31 January | Plain-language form (family-completed with help offered): student strengths and interests; communication; learning; social; care and medical needs; current placement; consent to obtain records. Attach IEP, any assessment, latest report card. **Families are told what information is needed and why; nothing more is requested** (OHRC: "the information requested must be the least intrusive of the person's privacy" — VERIFIED, OHRC policy position on medical documentation, accessed 2026-10-15). | Application file |
| 3. Records and conversation | February | Principal or coordinator meets the family (and the student, in whatever mode suits them); with consent, speaks to the current school/clinicians. | Intake notes |
| 4. Observation and trial days | March–April | One observation in the current setting (with consent) and **two to three trial days** in the prospective homeroom with the student's usual supports. Summit's intake model (v1 §9). The trial is to learn how to support the student, not to "test" them; the family is told that and receives feedback the same day. | Observation record (structured, domain-based) |
| 5. Intake committee | Late April–May | Principal (chair), the prospective homeroom teacher, an EA, the SLP or behaviour consultant as relevant, and one board-appointed member who is not staff. Applies section 2 and the group-viability tests; **considers what accommodation would let the school say yes** before saying no (the procedural duty — VERIFIED, OHRC 2018 policy §8: the duty "has both a substantive and a procedural component"). | Committee minute with reasons |
| 6. Written decision with reasons | Within 10 school days of the committee | Offer; conditional offer (with the condition named — e.g., "subject to the accessible washroom being complete"); waitlist (with position and the reason the place is not available now); or **"cannot meet needs at this time"** (section 4). All decisions name the review route. | Decision letter |
| 7. Transition-in | June–September | Visits, communication passport, personal care plan, behaviour plan, IEP drafted with the family before day one; staff training for any student-specific need. | IEP, care plan |
| 8. 90-day review | By the 90th school day | Formal meeting with the family: progress data, belonging indicators, what is and is not working, any change in supports or grouping. Mirrors the three-month review right under O. Reg. 181/98 (v1 §9). Continuation is the default; a documented transition-out plan is prepared only if the school and family agree the placement is not meeting the student's needs, and **no student leaves without a placement plan**. | Review record |

## 4. Accommodation obligations — what "cannot meet needs at this time" must look like

### 4.1 The law applies to this school

- **VERIFIED:** the OHRC *Policy on accessible education for students with disabilities* (revised version approved March 2018; replaces the 2004 Guidelines) states that the right to equal treatment and the duty to accommodate apply to "publicly and privately funded … elementary and secondary schools" (§3.2, "Applies to public and private education institutions"); executive summary: "This protection applies to elementary and secondary schools, and colleges and universities, both public and private." — https://www.ohrc.on.ca/en/policy-accessible-education-students-disabilities, accessed 2026-10-15.
- **VERIFIED:** education providers have "a legal obligation to accommodate students with disabilities up to the point of undue hardship" (§6), and the policy reaffirms "the high standard of undue hardship" (executive summary).
- **VERIFIED:** the duty is "informed by three principles" — respect for dignity, individualization, and integration and full participation (§8.1, §8.1.1–8.1.3); "students with disabilities are individuals first, and should be considered, assessed and accommodated on an individual basis"; "Privacy, confidentiality, comfort, individuality and self-esteem are all important factors" (§8.1.1); "accommodation is a responsibility shared by all parties to the process" and requires "meaningful dialogue" in which "everyone involved should co-operatively engage in the process, share information" (§8.3.1); "Information about accommodation procedures should be readily available to students" and parents (§8.3.2).
- **VERIFIED (Code s.17 as summarized in the policy §2.1.2):** a student "will only be considered incapable if their disability-related needs cannot be accommodated without undue hardship," and the provider "must also show that the needs of the student affected cannot be accommodated without undue hardship."
- **VERIFIED, with a tooling caveat:** the 2018 policy's §9 "Undue hardship" has exactly three sub-sections — 9.1.1 Costs, 9.1.2 Outside sources of funding, 9.1.3 Health and safety (table of contents of the PDF, p. 83, Mohawk College mirror, accessed 2026-10-15); the body text of §8.6–9 could not be retrieved (both the OHRC web page and the PDF mirror truncate at about 126,000 characters, ending in §8.3.2). The wording of the standard is therefore taken from the OHRC *Policy on ableism and discrimination based on disability* (approved 27 June 2016), §9, which the 2018 education policy applies:
  - "The Code prescribes only three considerations when assessing whether an accommodation would cause undue hardship … No other considerations can be properly taken into account under Ontario law." "Inconvenience by itself is not a factor." Business inconvenience, resentment or hostility of others, and customer/third-party preferences are not defences.
  - Onus and evidence: "the organization responsible for making the accommodation has the onus of proof"; evidence "must be objective, real, direct and, in the case of cost, quantifiable"; "A mere statement, without supporting evidence, that the cost or risk is 'too high'" based on speculation or stereotypes will not suffice.
  - Cost (§9.2.1): undue hardship only where costs are "so substantial that they would alter the essential nature of the enterprise, or so significant that they would substantially affect its viability"; "The cost standard is therefore a high one."
  - Outside sources of funding (§9.2.2): an organization "has an obligation to consider any outside sources of funding it can obtain" and must show "that they took advantage of any available government funding" before claiming cost.
  - Health and safety (§9.2.3): assessment "must reflect an accurate understanding of risk based on objective evidence rather than stereotypical views"; concerns that "might" or "could" occur, if speculative, do not sustain hardship; "the least intrusive means to address the risk must be used"; organizations "should apply a proportionate response" and consider dignity of risk.
  - Minimizing hardship (§9.3): "undue hardship should be avoided by phasing in the accessible features gradually"; "provide interim accommodation" while long-term accommodation proceeds; phasing and reserve funds are considered "only after" the provider shows the best accommodation "could not be accomplished immediately"; where part of an accommodation causes hardship, the organization "should determine if a less expensive or lower risk alternative exists" and "provision should be made for considering future changes in circumstances."
  — https://www.ohrc.on.ca/en/policy-ableism-and-discrimination-based-disability/9-undue-hardship, accessed 2026-10-15.
- **SECONDARY/CONFIRM:** the 2018 policy's own §9 text and §8.6 ("Duties and responsibilities in the accommodation process"), §8.7 ("Medical information to be provided") and §8.8 ("Confidentiality and protecting disability-related information") should be read in full from a complete copy (print PDF from the OHRC site) before the admissions policy is adopted. Their headings are VERIFIED; their contents are assumed to track the 2016 ableism policy.
- **VERIFIED (OHRC policy position on medical documentation, referencing ableism policy §8.7):** an accommodation provider is generally entitled to know "that the person has a disability," "the limitations or needs associated with the disability," and "the type of accommodation(s) that may be needed"; a diagnosis is generally not required — the focus "should always be on the functional limitations"; requests must be "the least intrusive of the person's privacy"; "Overbroad requests for private medical information" undermine dignity. More information is appropriate only when "the person's needs are complex, challenging or unclear." — https://www.ohrc.on.ca/en/ohrc-policy-position-medical-documentation-be-provided-when-disability-related-accommodation, accessed 2026-10-15. **Implication for intake:** the application asks for needs and supports; a psychological assessment is *requested if available* because it describes learning needs, but its absence is not a bar, and the school never requires a diagnosis label as a condition of consideration.
- **VERIFIED (OHRC fact sheet, roles and responsibilities):** all education providers must "investigate accommodation solutions and grant accommodation requests in a timely manner"; each party must "share information, and canvass potential accommodation solutions"; the student "or his or her parent or guardian" must advise of the need, participate in discussions and "work with the accommodation provider on an ongoing basis." — https://www.ohrc.on.ca/en/accommodating-students-disabilities-roles-and-responsibilities-fact-sheet, accessed 2026-10-15 (undated).
- **VERIFIED (AODA, Integrated Accessibility Standards — customer service, organizations with 1–19 and 20–49 employees):** the school must allow assistive devices; welcome service animals and support persons; provide notice of temporary disruptions; create accessible ways to give feedback ("accessible comment cards or surveys"); "upon request, provide accessible formats and communication supports"; and "train all your employees and volunteers on" the Human Rights Code and accessible customer service. At 20–49 employees an accessibility compliance report is due ("Deadline to file an accessibility compliance report is December 31, 2026" for the current cycle). — https://www.ontario.ca/page/accessibility-rules-businesses-and-non-profits, updated 16 June 2026, accessed 2026-10-15. The 50-student staffing plan (≈24 FTE, more headcount) puts the school in the 20–49 band from opening (ESTIMATE; employee count includes part-time and contract workers, not volunteers — v1 legal note VERIFIED).
- **VERIFIED (Human Rights Code s.14 special programs):** see section 1, point 3.

### 4.2 What a defensible "cannot meet needs at this time" decision contains (PROPOSAL built on the VERIFIED standard)

The intake committee may conclude the school cannot meet a student's needs only when the file shows all of the following:

1. **Individualized assessment** — the five domains and both group tests applied to *this* student from the application, records, conversation and trial days; no reliance on a label, an IQ score, a syndrome, age, or another family's experience.
2. **Accommodation was actually considered** — the minute names the accommodations canvassed (e.g., additional EA hours, altered grouping, SLP time, equipment, phased start, partial-week start as an *interim* step only) and why each is or is not available now, with the resource gap stated in concrete terms (which role, equipment or training from `educational-design.md` §8).
3. **Outside funding was checked** — the file records which family-directed or public sources were explored for the specific gap (e.g., Ontario Health atHome nursing hours; ADP for communication devices; SSAH/OAP for non-tuition supports) and the result, because the OHRC requires providers to consider outside sources before claiming cost (VERIFIED above). The public-services agent's findings feed this list.
4. **Health and safety claims rest on evidence** — incident records, current plans and clinician input, not on what "could" happen; a proportionate, least-intrusive alternative was considered.
5. **Written reasons** in plain language to the family within 10 school days, stating: the needs identified; the accommodations considered; the specific resource the school lacks; what would change the answer (and whether the school is planning it); the right to a review by a board member not on the committee within 20 school days; and an open invitation to re-apply at any intake or when circumstances change.
6. **No partial-day exclusion as a standing arrangement.** Asking a family to keep a student home for part of the day is the practice the OHRC's 2018 policy sought to end (v1 §2, VERIFIED via People for Education summary) and the P4E data show it is driven mainly by safety and unavailable supports (73% / 18%, VERIFIED). The school may use a phased or reduced start only as a documented, time-limited interim accommodation with a date for full attendance.
7. **A transition plan for any current student** the school can no longer serve: the school continues to serve the student while the family secures another placement (the public board must admit a resident pupil), shares records with consent, and does not withdraw services as leverage.

A decision file lacking any of 1–5 is not defensible and should not be issued.

## 5. Waitlist and priority rules (PROPOSAL)

Places are offered in this order, applied only among applicants who meet the section 2 criteria and the compatible-homeroom test:

1. **Returning students** and students already on a conditional offer whose condition is now met.
2. **Siblings** of enrolled students (family logistics; shared transport; belonging) — a common and defensible preference in Ontario private schools and in public-school attendance policies.
3. **Catchment** — residence within the Belleville hub and the Trenton–Stirling–Tyendinaga service area (defined as the transport agent's 45-minute zone; FOUNDER brief). Students outside the zone are eligible but ranked after those inside, because the school cannot transport them and distance predicts attendance.
4. **Date of complete application** — the earlier complete application ranks first; "complete" means the form and consent, not the assessment, so a family waiting on a public assessment is not penalized.
5. **Group balance** as a tie-break only — where two applicants are otherwise equal, the one whose profile keeps a homeroom within its staffing rules (e.g., not a third student needing intensive behaviour support in a group of eight) is offered first, and the other is told exactly why and when the next compatible place is expected.

Explicitly **not** used, at any step: predicted productivity, employability or independence trajectory; perceived "potential" or likeability; IQ or adaptive score thresholds; continence status; diagnosis label; family wealth, donation history or willingness to fundraise or volunteer; parental occupation; religion or any other Code ground; whether the student "would do better here than at the public school" (an outcome prediction the school cannot make — v1 §2).

Waitlist mechanics: written position and expected-timing letter twice a year; places that open mid-year go to the first compatible applicant in rank order; the list resets annually with a one-form re-confirmation so stale entries do not block others; the board receives an anonymized annual report of applications, offers, declines, waitlist and "cannot meet needs" decisions by domain, so the pattern of refusals is visible and reviewable.

## 6. Privacy of intake and student records (brief)

- **MFIPPA/FIPPA do not apply** to a private school; they govern public bodies (SECONDARY — v1 legal note; not re-researched).
- **PIPEDA:** the Office of the Privacy Commissioner found that a charitable, not-for-profit private school collecting personal information for admissions "was not engaged in any commercial activities": where "the core activity is providing educational services," those activities "should presumptively be considered not to have a 'commercial character'," rebutted only if an objective is "earning a profit for the owners of the institution" — **VERIFIED**, PIPEDA Case Summary #2006-345, findings 5 July 2006, accessed 2026-10-15. The OPC's Interpretation Bulletin on commercial activity (issued 2017-01-30, modified 2022-03-24) repeats that "Not-for-profit organizations are not automatically exempt from PIPEDA," that tax status is not determinative, and that selling or leasing "donor, membership or other fundraising lists" is commercial — **VERIFIED**, accessed 2026-10-15. **Implication:** a tuition-free charitable school is presumptively outside PIPEDA for admissions and student records, but fundraising-list transactions and any fee-for-service activity (e.g., a Passport-billed service, market sales data) could be commercial. **PROPOSAL:** adopt PIPEDA's ten fair-information principles as policy anyway (consent, limiting collection, safeguards, access, retention) — it costs little and insurers and funders expect it.
- **PHIPA:** a school is not a health information custodian unless it operates a health program whose primary purpose is health care; "It depends on the way the relationship between the school and the health care practitioner is structured"; a visiting practitioner "may be an individual health care practitioner who provides the service to the school" (custodian in their own right); contracts "should set out who is the custodian," and students and parents should know who that is; a school that is not the custodian "cannot collect, use or disclose a student's health number" except in limited cases — **VERIFIED**, IPC Ontario, *Health Privacy Law in Schools* (Dara Lambie, 28 Jan 2022), accessed 2026-10-15. **Implications:** the contracted SLP, OT, BCBA-supervised behaviour service and psychologist will each be custodians (or agents of their own practice) for their clinical records; the school holds only what they disclose for educational purposes, and a recipient's use and disclosure of personal health information received from a custodian is restricted to the purpose for which it was disclosed (PHIPA s.49 — **CONFIRM**, statute not read; e-Laws JavaScript-gated). Service agreements must name the custodian, where records live, and what the school receives.
- **CYFSA duty to report** overrides confidentiality and PHIPA (v1 legal note, VERIFIED).
- **Practical rules (PROPOSAL):** separate the intake file (application, assessments, decisions) from the day-to-day student record; lock physical files, encrypt digital ones, and limit access to the intake committee and the student's homeroom team; retain intake files of non-admitted applicants for one year then destroy unless the family asks to stay on the waitlist; give families access to their file on request; never hold health-card numbers; consent forms for each third-party records request. **Repository rule:** no identifiable child or family record ever enters this public repository (CLAUDE.md).

## 7. Students with Down syndrome

Students with Down syndrome apply, are observed, and are decided through sections 2–5 exactly as any other applicant. The intake committee neither presumes fit (most students with Down syndrome have an intellectual disability in the mild-to-moderate range, and the v1 evidence shows they profit from explicit reading instruction — Allor et al. 2014 included Down syndrome, VERIFIED) nor presumes a particular communication or medical profile (congenital heart conditions, hearing loss and sleep apnea are common but individual — SECONDARY, general clinical knowledge, not researched here). Advocacy positions that favour inclusive placement (CDSS 2020, VERIFIED v1 §2) are respected: the information session states plainly that the school is a congregated setting, that the evidence does not show congregated settings produce better outcomes, and that the choice is the family's.
